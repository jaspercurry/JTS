## TL;DR

- **The hard 350 Hz ceiling is not defensible as a hard wall, but the doctrine behind it is largely right.** The serious field splits into two camps: modal-only correctors (Genelec, Neumann, JBL SFM — "the room doesn't create problems above ~500 Hz") and full-range correctors (Dirac, Trinnov, Anthem, Audyssey) that only extend up high with *broad, low-Q, gentle* filters and phase tools JTS's IIR layer doesn't have. **Recommendation: raise JTS's room ceiling from a hard 350 Hz to a per-room Schroeder-derived transition (clamped 250–500 Hz) for full-resolution correction, then permit only broad low-Q / shelf / tilt "room-power" trend correction of the *residual* (in-room minus gated) from that point up to ~1 kHz, capped hard. Above ~1 kHz the room layer does nothing.**
- **The two-instrument problem is solved by attribution, not bandwidth.** Because JTS uniquely already owns the speaker's anechoic/gated response full-band, the room layer above the transition should correct only the *difference* between the spatially-averaged in-room curve and the gated curve — i.e., only what the room added — exactly the philosophy Genelec/Neumann apply by voicing to an anechoically-informed target. This is the single biggest competitive differentiator available to JTS and prevents double-correction by construction.
- **Phase/FIR correction is not worth its cost on this hardware for a single-area domestic speaker.** Group-delay audibility thresholds (below 1.0 ms from 300 Hz–1 kHz is inaudible, per Liski/Mäkivirta/Välimäki) are far above what minimum-phase IIR already achieves; the gated layer already time-aligns the drivers; FIR adds unacceptable latency. Notably, even Anthem — a "full-range" product — is IIR-only by deliberate design. Keep the room layer minimum-phase IIR and spend the budget on 8 phone-cloud positions (diminishing returns beyond, per the 1/√N averaging law).

## Key Findings

**1. The field divides cleanly into two doctrines about the transition frequency.** The *studio-monitor school* (Genelec, Neumann, JBL Synthesis SFM) restricts correction to the modal region. Genelec's own AutoCal "applies relevant compensation in the low and low-mid frequencies"; a Genelec-adjacent engineer (Chaikin, on the related Intonato 24) put the doctrine bluntly: "The room really doesn't create problems for monitors above 500 Hz… Low frequencies are where the room tends to be in control." The consumer/AVR school (Dirac, Audyssey, Anthem, Trinnov) offers full-range correction but hedges it heavily with defaults, tilts, and phase machinery.

**2. Even the "full-range" products default to modal-only or blend out gently up high.** Anthem ARC Genesis defaults its Maximum Correction Frequency to 5,000 Hz (user-extendable to 20 kHz); Anthem's Peter Schuck stated in a SoundStage! Access interview: "By default, ARC Genesis will not EQ above 5kHz, but the user can now force EQ changes in the uppermost frequencies, up to 20kHz." His colleague added: "there's always a limit at some point where you have to blend back to the in-room passband response of the speaker." Dirac ships full-range but its "curtains" let users limit to the modal region, and both its documentation and community consensus recommend limiting to ~300–500 Hz unless phase correction is active. Audyssey XT32 is capable of 10 Hz–24 kHz but expert practice (and the MultEQ Editor app) routinely limits it to ~500 Hz.

**3. The literature says above-transition tonal problems are a speaker property, not a room property — and are not minimum-phase correctable.** Toole: "it is comprehensive anechoic measurements that are definitive of sound quality at frequencies above the transition/Schroeder frequency (often 300–400 Hz) not the steady state room curve." The shape of the mid/HF room curve "is dominated by off-axis radiated 'early reflections' and that is a property of loudspeakers that cannot be equalized." This directly validates JTS's gated-layer-owns-the-speaker architecture.

**4. But spatially-averaged *broad* correction up high measurably helps — when it corrects a real trend.** Olive's AES room-correction evaluations found the most-preferred corrections produced "the smoothest, most extended in-room responses measured around the primary listening seat," and used spatial averaging specifically "to avoid equalizing spectral artifacts that are very localized to the position of the microphone." The preferred in-room target is not flat but a gentle downward tilt (roughly 5–8 dB bass-to-treble in a reflective room; ~2–3 dB/decade in Toole's reference curve).

**5. Nulls remain uncorrectable; peaks are minimum-phase and correctable.** REW's own documentation: "the peaks in a response are a result of features that are correctable through equalisation" while nulls from destructive interference "cannot be restored to what it should have been by any amount of EQ." Toole: "Room resonances at low frequencies behave as 'minimum phase' phenomena, and so, if the amplitude vs. frequency characteristic is corrected, so also [is the phase]." Practitioner consensus: cut peaks freely, limit boost into dips to ~6 dB, never chase deep narrow nulls.

**6. Phase/excess-phase correction has thin audibility support for this use case.** Group-delay audibility: Liski, Mäkivirta & Välimäki (AES 144th Convention, Milan, May 2018) found "when the group delay in the frequency range from 300 Hz to 1 kHz is below 1.0 ms, it is inaudible"; their 2021 follow-up ("Audibility of Group-Delay Equalization") refined critical-signal thresholds to sub-millisecond (−0.56 ms negative, 0.64 ms positive with a pink impulse). Music masks most of it. Only Dirac and Trinnov ship mixed-phase/FIR correction; Anthem is deliberately IIR-only; and Sonos explicitly *chooses not to* correct phase: "if you take the DFT of a system's impulse response, you get its frequency response, phase vs. frequency as well as amplitude vs. frequency. For the spectral correction, we don't use the phase information." The room's non-minimum-phase behavior can only be corrected at one point, degrading everywhere else.

**7. Spatial averaging: ~5–8 positions captures most of the benefit.** Harman/Toole standard is 5–6 seats; Audyssey uses up to 8; Dirac uses 3 (quick) to 9 (full), with dealer guidance of "5–7 measurements with small incremental gains from more." Averaging error converges as 1/√N (Cengarle & Mateos, AES 136, Paper 9083), so gains beyond ~8 are marginal for a single listening area. The moving-microphone method (MMM) is equivalent to a dense multipoint average (Peace et al., AES 144, Paper 9960) and is effectively what Sonos Trueplay does (150+ frames over 45 s).

### The literature, condensed

- **Toole / Olive direct-vs-steady-state:** Below the transition the room dominates (a resonator); above it, direct sound and the speaker's own directivity dominate perception, and the ear "hears through" mid/HF room artifacts. Steady-state room curves above transition should be a *result*, not a *target* — a gentle downward tilt for a good speaker. Equalizing an irregular mid/HF room curve flat "guarantees nothing" because off-axis energy is a speaker property that EQ cannot fix.
- **Schroeder frequency:** In small domestic rooms the transition is typically ~100–500 Hz (commonly cited ~200–400 Hz); it is lower in more damped rooms. It is a transition *region* — extending to roughly 4× the Schroeder frequency (the "diffusion zone," ~600 Hz in many rooms) — not a brick wall.
- **Minimum-phase correctability:** Modal peaks are minimum-phase → correcting magnitude also fixes ringing/phase. Nulls (SBIR, destructive interference) are non-minimum-phase → uncorrectable by EQ; treat with placement/multi-sub. Acoustic Frontiers' measurements ("Hard proof that equalization kills room modes") confirm EQ shortens modal ringing at peaks.
- **MMM practice:** Move the mic slowly over a ~0.5 m volume around the seat with continuous RTA averaging; repeatable and equivalent to a dense spatial average. Above transition, smoothing/averaging masks nulls (expected and desirable).
- **Multi-sub / spatial variance (Welti & Devantier, JAES 54(5), May 2006):** "not using bass management can result in seat-to-seat variations of 40 dB or more at some frequencies." Four subs at wall midpoints is the practical optimum; two at opposing midpoints nearly as good; configurations with more than four "were not found to be advantageous." Sound Field Management optimization lowered mean spatial variance by ~13.9 dB² (2 subs) to ~16.6 dB² (4 subs) versus a single subwoofer. (Relevant to JTS only if it ships multi-driver LF or stereo-pair bass.)

### FIR cost on the JTS target (Raspberry Pi 5, CamillaDSP f64)

CamillaDSP uses FFT convolution (FFT length = 2× chunksize) and its own benchmark reports a **Raspberry Pi 4** doing **8 channels × 262k taps @ 192 kHz at ~55% CPU**. The Pi 5 is materially faster, so raw CPU is not the binding constraint — **latency and complexity are.** A linear-phase FIR with useful resolution near 200 Hz needs on the order of several thousand taps at 48 kHz (frequency resolution ≈ Fs/N; ~5 Hz resolution ≈ 9,600 taps), and linear-phase latency is ~N/2 samples (≈100 ms at 9,600 taps/48 kHz) — unacceptable for a smart speaker with lip-sync and voice-assistant duties. Anthem's designers independently cite roughly a one-second penalty as the reason they stay IIR-only. Minimum-phase FIR reduces latency but then offers little over IIR biquads, which are essentially free here. **Verdict: FIR/excess-phase correction is not justified on this target for a single-area domestic speaker.**

## Recommendations (adopted-decisions, with deltas from the current 20–350 Hz PEQ regime)

**DECISION 1 — Bandwidth: ADOPT a two-tier ceiling replacing the hard 350 Hz wall.**
- **Tier A (full-resolution modal correction):** 20 Hz up to a **per-room transition frequency f_t**, computed per-room (Schroeder estimate from the measured decay, clamped to **[250 Hz, 500 Hz]**, default 350 Hz if estimation is uncertain). *Delta:* the ceiling becomes evidence-derived per-room, not a hard-coded 350.
- **Tier B (broad room-power trend only):** From f_t up to a hard stop of **1,000 Hz**, permit only broad, low-Q correction of the *residual room-power trend* (see Decision 2). *Delta:* a new region the room layer gains — subject to the full honesty/verify/rollback machinery.
- **Above 1 kHz: REJECT room correction entirely.** The gated layer owns it; the ear hears direct sound; sparse-cloud data is unreliable there. This matches Genelec/Neumann/JBL doctrine and Anthem's 5 kHz default intent.

**DECISION 2 — Two-instrument attribution: ADOPT "correct only what the room added" above f_t.**
- In Tier B, the room layer's correction target is the **difference between the spatially-averaged in-room curve and the gated (anechoic-equivalent) curve** the speaker layer already measured — not the raw in-room curve. This subtract-the-anechoic approach is exactly what Neumann's Fraunhofer target does (it "takes into account the anechoic data") and is JTS's unique structural advantage, since it already possesses the gated response. It makes double-correction impossible *by construction* rather than by a shared constant. *Delta:* the room layer never re-flattens speaker voicing that layer 1 intentionally set.
- Filter vocabulary in Tier B: **low-shelf / high-shelf and tilt terms, plus broad PEQ with Q ≤ 1.0**; **gain cap ±3 dB per term and ±4 dB net** across the band. No narrow-Q filters above f_t.

**DECISION 3 — The 140–350 Hz shared band: ADOPT gated-layer primacy with a documented handoff.**
- Both instruments see this band. The **gated layer owns absolute tonal balance** (it is the more reliable, room-deleted measurement); the **room layer owns only modal peak-cutting and broad trend** here, working on the residual. Handoff frequency = f_valid (~143 Hz): below it, the room layer + bass-extension own everything (gated is invalid); from f_valid to f_t both contribute but the room is residual-only. This mirrors RoomPerfect's Focus (direct/speaker) vs. room split and Neumann's anechoic-informed target.

**DECISION 4 — Target curve: ADOPT flat-modal + speaker's-own-voicing; tilt lives in Preference.**
- The room layer targets **flat in the modal region relative to the speaker's designed voicing** (it removes room deviation, not speaker character). The **house tilt (Harman-style, ~ −0.5 to −1 dB/oct) lives in the Preference layer**, user-editable, shipped as a gentle default. This matches Lyngdorf (RoomPerfect removes room; Voicing adds taste) and keeps a clean separation. *Delta:* no change to room-layer LF target philosophy; formalizes that tilt is NOT the room layer's job.

**DECISION 5 — LF boost policy: ADOPT "boost minimum-phase dips within headroom, gated by the null registry."**
- Cut modal peaks freely. Boost into dips **only where the null registry confirms the dip is minimum-phase (modal) and not an interference null**, cap **+6 dB**, and only within measured amplifier/excursion headroom, with closed-loop verify-and-rollback. This is the defensible modern line (Anthem's algorithm applies 0 to +6 dB with null-avoidance; practitioner consensus is ≤6 dB). *Delta:* replaces any blanket cut-only policy at LF with evidence-gated boosts — now safe because JTS has the null registry that Anthem and others only approximate.

**DECISION 6 — Phase: REJECT FIR/excess-phase room correction on this hardware.**
- Keep the room layer **minimum-phase IIR biquads**. Group-delay audibility thresholds (~1 ms and below, 300 Hz–1 kHz) exceed what IIR leaves; the gated layer already time-aligns drivers; FIR latency (~100 ms for useful LF resolution, ~1 s in Anthem's framing) breaks smart-speaker duties; the room's non-min-phase errors are only correctable at one point. Sonos and Anthem independently reached the same "magnitude-only / IIR-only" conclusion. *Delta:* closes the FIR question with a quantified rejection.

**DECISION 7 — Spatial protocol: ADOPT an 8-position guided phone cloud with power-averaging (already largely in place).**
- Target **8 positions** for the listening area (diminishing returns beyond, per the 1/√N convergence law and Audyssey/Harman practice), power-averaged with the existing outlier/interference screen. Offer a **quick 3–4 position** mode (Dirac-style) for casual users. Consider an **MMM option** for UMIK-2 users (equivalent to a dense average; what Sonos does). *Delta:* formalizes 8 as the "diminishing-returns" default with named justification.

### Pre-registered predictions (testable with JTS's existing instruments)

1. **Tier-B residual correction improves the in-room average without harming the gated curve.** A broad ≤2 dB, Q≤1 room-power correction over f_t–1 kHz, derived from the 8-position cloud residual, will reduce the spatially-averaged in-room deviation from target by ≥1.5 dB RMS in that band while degrading the gated (quasi-anechoic) curve by ≤0.5 dB RMS. *Rollback trigger:* gated degradation >0.5 dB RMS.
2. **Per-room f_t beats fixed 350 Hz.** In rooms where the measured Schroeder estimate is <300 Hz or >400 Hz, using the per-room f_t will lower post-correction spatial variance in the 200–600 Hz band by ≥1 dB² vs. the fixed-350 regime, with no listener-preference regression.
3. **8 positions ≈ 12 positions.** The power-averaged curve from 8 positions will differ from a 12-position average by ≤0.75 dB RMS across 20 Hz–1 kHz (confirming diminishing returns and validating 8 as default).
4. **Null-registry-gated boosts are safe.** Boosts ≤6 dB into registry-confirmed minimum-phase dips will improve in-room flatness at those frequencies by the applied amount ±1 dB on remeasurement, whereas boosts attempted into registry-flagged interference nulls will fail to improve (≤1 dB change) — validating the registry gate.
5. **Minimum-phase IIR leaves no audible group-delay penalty.** Measured post-correction excess group delay in 300 Hz–1 kHz will remain <1 ms (below the Liski/Välimäki audibility threshold), confirming FIR phase correction is unnecessary.

## Caveats

- **Product internals are often marketing-opaque.** Lyngdorf RoomPerfect and Apple HomePod are effectively black boxes; RoomPerfect internals here are inferred from official concept docs and reviews, and HomePod behavior is community inference only (LOW confidence). Trinnov/Dirac mixed-phase details come from official docs that emphasize marketing benefits.
- **"Full-range" is a marketing default, not an evidence-based recommendation.** Every full-range product either defaults to modal-only intent (Anthem 5 kHz, Genelec ~500 Hz) or is hedged by expert practice limiting to ~500 Hz. Distinguish what ships (full-range capability) from what the evidence supports (broad, gentle, residual-only above the transition).
- **The Cengarle & Mateos exact "diminishing-returns N" is paywalled;** the 1/√N convergence law is confirmed, and the 5–8 position figure is a synthesis of converging product guidance (Dirac 5–7, Harman 5–6, Audyssey 8) plus that law.
- **Welti MSV figures are in dB² (variance), not dB,** and apply to multi-subwoofer scenarios — directly relevant only if JTS ships multi-driver or stereo-pair LF handling.
- **Group-delay thresholds vary with signal and level;** ~1 ms is a conservative critical-signal figure. Low-frequency phase can be audible at high levels on very capable systems, but not for a single domestic smart speaker at normal levels.
- **The phone-mic tier limits Tier-B confidence.** Uncalibrated phone mics are least reliable in exactly the 500 Hz–1 kHz region; Tier-B correction should be more conservative (or disabled) on the phone tier than on the UMIK-2, with explicit user-facing disclosure — consistent with the honesty machinery. Sonos mitigates this by per-device-model microphone calibration curves, an option unavailable to JTS across the open Android field, reinforcing conservatism on the phone tier.

---

### Bottom line for the owner
The current 350 Hz ceiling is *conservative but not wrong in spirit* — the evidence overwhelmingly supports gated-layer/anechoic ownership of the speaker's mid/HF response and warns against narrow-Q flattening of the steady-state curve up high. Where the doctrine is beatable is at the **margins and in attribution**: (a) make the ceiling a per-room transition rather than a hard 350; (b) add a strictly-bounded Tier-B "room-power residual" region from f_t to 1 kHz that corrects *only what the room added on top of the known anechoic response* — a move that is genuinely more principled than what any shipping competitor does, precisely because JTS already holds the gated curve; and (c) unlock evidence-gated LF boosts now that the null registry exists. Reject FIR/phase correction outright on this hardware. These deltas make JTS competitive with Dirac/Anthem/Trinnov on capability while remaining more defensible than any of them on doctrine.
