Quasi-Anechoic Loudspeaker Measurement & EQ: A Decision-Grade Technical Report

TL;DR

* Your +2.2 dB boost at 410 Hz (≈2.9 cycles in a 7 ms gate, just above your own 357 Hz trusted floor) is unsupportable as currently justified. At 1 m in a small room, 410 Hz sits in the transition band where a floor/ceiling-bounce SBIR null is the single most likely cause of a dip there, and SBIR nulls are non-minimum-phase cancellations that EQ cannot fix and that waste headroom. Do not apply it until you corroborate it with a near-field/ground-plane measurement, a minimum-phase (excess-group-delay) check, and spatial averaging.
* On the central contested question (fixed gate vs. frequency-dependent window), the honest state of the art is unresolved and the correct engineering answer is exactly your instinct: compute both and treat disagreement as information. REW's author John Mulcahy states FDW and variable smoothing are "not equivalent" but that plain smoothing "provides better EQ targets"; Brueggemann/Acourate and Audiolense build FDW into their correction as psychoacoustically motivated. There is no peer-reviewed head-to-head that settles it.
* "Multi-gate convergence" (trust only features stable across gate lengths) is sound and has strong analogues in the statistics literature (multitaper jackknife, Welch consistency), but it is not a named, formalized technique in any loudspeaker tool. You would be formalizing something practitioners do informally — a legitimate contribution, not a reinvention.

Key Findings

1. The 2.5/T–3/T "trusted floor" convention is a practical safety margin layered on top of the rigorous rule fmin = 1/T. The primary sources (D'Appolito, ARTA/Mateljan) state the hard limit as one cycle per window (1/T), below which data is "simply an artifact of the Fourier Transform." No primary source tabulates a dB error at 2, 2.5, or 3 cycles; the 2–3× multiplier is engineering prudence, not a quantified law. Your 357 Hz floor at 2.5/T is defensible and conservative — but 410 Hz is barely above it.
2. FDW is a constant-Q / wavelet-like time-frequency tradeoff. It is mathematically near-identical to complex (frequency-dependent) smoothing in its magnitude effect, but it is not identical to variable smoothing, and it discards late-arriving energy rather than averaging it. Its low-frequency behavior necessarily reduces resolution exactly where you need modal information — the core of the "FDW throws away the room data you need for EQ" objection.
3. Below the gate floor, you have three truth-generating options, each with hard validity ceilings: near-field (valid to ka<1, i.e., fmax ≈ 4311/D_inches, mic within 0.11×radius), ground-plane (6 dB gain, mic on floor, half the distance), and Klippel NFS (holographic field separation, true anechoic in an ordinary room). All three give 2π/half-space loading and must have baffle-step diffraction added back before splicing.
4. Minimum-phase testing via excess group delay (Hilbert-derived) is the established, correct method, implemented in REW, Acourate, and others. The key theoretical result (Neely & Allen 1979; Toole; Karjalainen et al.) is that single-point modal peaks are usually minimum-phase and correctable, while dips from cancellation (SBIR, multi-mode summation) are generally not minimum-phase and are futile/harmful to boost.

Details

Question 1 — Fixed gate vs. frequency-dependent window (FDW)

What FDW actually is. A frequency-dependent window applies, at each frequency, a time window whose width is a fixed number of cycles of that frequency. REW's help: "a 15 cycle window will have a width at 1 kHz of 15 × (1/1000) = 0.015 s or 15 ms," and "The corresponding octave fraction has an effect similar to applying a smoothing of the same octave fraction, except the variable window excludes progressively more of the late arriving sound as frequency increases rather than just averaging it out — this has similarities with the way the ear increasingly picks out the direct sound from the speaker at higher frequencies." Acourate's wiki (audiovero.de) describes it identically: "at every frequency it accepts a window of time that is specified as a number of cycles at that frequency and ignores any portion of the signal at that frequency that occurs outside the time window." This is a constant-Q, wavelet-like tiling of the time-frequency plane — the low-frequency window is long (to capture modes), the high-frequency window is short (to reject reflections). REW implements it as a frequency-dependent Gaussian window centred on the impulse peak.

THE STRONGEST CASE FOR FDW (Brueggemann/Acourate, Audiolense, OCA, WiiM):

* Psychoacoustic motivation. Brueggemann's stated design intent (as relayed in the REW feature-request thread on hometheatershack.com) is that "15 cycles… provides sufficient psychoacoustic width for low frequencies and sufficiently 'anechoic' width for the ear at high frequencies." Acourate pairs FDW with a proprietary "psychoacoustic frequency response" to produce a "mild correction" that "is listenable without problems… all over the room." The claim is that the ear itself integrates over a roughly constant number of cycles, so an FDW'd response better models what is heard than any fixed window.
* It reveals features a fixed window hides. Mulcahy (AV NIRVANA): "The FDW gets narrower as frequency increases, so things that may be filled in by later reflections become visible."
* It produces spatially robust corrections. Because FDW concentrates correction "close around to the first sound arrival" (digitalroomcorrection.hk quoting Brueggemann), it avoids the classic failure mode where "an old style correction which heavily boosts a dip at the listening position will create a boom at another position."
* Practitioners including OCA (the A1 Evo / AcoustiX suite for Denon/Marantz + Dirac) and reviewer Archimago rely on FDW as implemented in Acourate, Audiolense, and Dirac; Archimago describes it as "a sliding window technique that scales the analysis and correction progressively across the frequency range… low frequencies… require the analysis window to be longer like say 750ms at 20Hz compared to only 15ms at 1kHz," arguing it "correlates with how our ears/brain integrate frequencies."

THE STRONGEST CASE AGAINST FDW / FOR FIXED GATE + SEPARATE SMOOTHING (Mulcahy's practical stance; signal-theory objections):

* Mulcahy's own verdict is that they are not equivalent and smoothing is better for EQ. Verbatim (HTS FDW thread): "Variable windowing and variable smoothing are not equivalent, but smoothing provides better EQ targets from the tests I have done." He notes "complex smoothing is mathematically equivalent to variable windowing," but that "Variable windowing on its own isn't enough to give a psycho-acoustic response, other factors need to be taken into account such as removing or reducing narrow dips, which remain present in variable windowing." When he added EQ-target-match for FDW'd responses he still cautioned: "smoothing is recommended if applying EQ above a couple of hundred Hz."
* FDW discards exactly the low-frequency room information you need for EQ. By construction, FDW's inherent smoothing grows toward low frequencies (Mulcahy: "as frequency increases the window gets narrower and so frequency resolution is reduced correspondingly"; and, on tuning it, "The narrower (in cycles) the FDW is the more it will attenuate reflections and their effect on the response, but the stronger the smoothing effect and the lower the resolution. The generated minimum phase will also be affected by the reflections"). Even the low-frequency FDW window, if set to a modest cycle count, truncates modal ringtails — and modal decay is the very thing you correct below Schroeder. A 15-cycle window at 40 Hz is 375 ms (preserves modes); a 5-cycle window at 40 Hz is only 125 ms and begins cutting long modal tails. The setting matters enormously and there is no consensus value.
* An FDW'd magnitude is not the transfer function of any realizable LTI system unless handled carefully. FDW is a time-varying (frequency-dependent) operation on the impulse response; the resulting magnitude curve no longer corresponds to a single causal, minimum-phase-recoverable LTI transfer function, and the min-phase reconstruction Mulcahy warns "will also be affected by the reflections" retained. Deriving an inverse EQ from an FDW'd curve therefore mixes a psychoacoustic display transform with a physical correction target — a category error if done naively.
* Real-world regression. WiiM's FDW-based "Precision" room correction drew user reports (forum.wiimhome.com) that "with 'Precision' RC (FDW) enabled the filters are less sharp… it is apparent right away that the 'Precision' RC has significantly less bass… kick drums and bass lines lose a lot of their impact." This is the predicted consequence of FDW's low-frequency smoothing reducing the depth of modal cuts/boosts.

Commercial approaches for context. Dirac Live uses a "mixed-phase" FIR+IIR approach that corrects time-domain (impulse) behavior, not a cycle-window display transform, and its own white paper limits correction where "a zero outside the unit circle… moves a lot with position" (a robustness criterion, not FDW). Trinnov emphasizes correcting in both time and frequency domains. These are different philosophies from the FDW-as-measurement-transform camp.

Is there a published resolution? No. There is no peer-reviewed, controlled comparison of "fixed gate + smoothing" vs. "FDW" as EQ-target generators with listening-test endpoints. The academic room-EQ literature (Room Response Equalization—A Review, MDPI 2018) frames the field around minimum- vs. mixed-phase equalizer design and multipoint averaging, not the FDW display/target transform. Honest state of knowledge: genuinely unresolved.

Assessment & recommendation for your pipeline. Your instinct to defer FDW as "disputed" is correct, and the strongest defensible design is exactly compute both and treat disagreement as information. Concretely: generate (a) your fixed-gate response at the 3/5/7 ms ladder, (b) an FDW'd response at a low and a high cycle count (e.g., 5 and 15 cycles), and (c) a variable/psychoacoustic-smoothed response. Where fixed-gate, FDW, and smoothed all agree on a feature, treat it as real and correctable. Where FDW "fills in" a dip that the fixed gate shows deep (the classic floor-bounce case — one HTS user found "the floor suckout is at 400hz… With the right window at 500ms and the FDW at 15 cycles the suckout is visible… Change the FDW to 5 cycles and the suckout is apparent in both speakers"), that disagreement is the diagnostic signal that the dip is reflection-caused and must not be boosted. Do not use an FDW'd curve as the direct EQ target below a few hundred Hz — follow Mulcahy's practice of using smoothing for EQ targets.

Question 2 — Validity rules for windowed magnitude estimates

The rigorous rule is fmin = 1/T. D'Appolito (author of Testing Loudspeakers), in his CLIO low-frequency article: "By using only 3.54 msec of data in this example the lowest frequency we can resolve is fmin = 1/0.00354 = 282 Hz. Any part of the curve plotted below that frequency is simply an artifact of the Fourier Transform and does not represent valid data." ARTA (Mateljan, App Note 4) states the same "time-bandwidth requirement": the validity bar sits at 1/gate (e.g., "1/gate = 1000/5.604 = 178.4" Hz for a 5.6 ms gate), and "the gate length should be 5 ms or more… for correct frequency response estimation on frequencies above 200 Hz." Klippel AN39 likewise: a 3 ms far-field window "provides meaningful data above 300 Hz."

Why the 1/T limit exists physically. To estimate a sinusoid's magnitude by FFT, at least one full period must fit inside the window; the FFT bin spacing is 1/T, and this resolution is constant in Hz across the whole band — a subtle point that trips people up. As Marek Bok put it in a detailed diyAudio thread: "With a 5 ms gate there is basically the same amount of information between 0–200 Hz as is between 200 and 400 Hz… At 10 kHz it is just a very small fraction of an octave. Not so at 400 Hz. This simply comes from the FFT and there's nothing you can do with that."

Where does 2.5/T–3/T come from, and is it defensible or folklore? It is a practical safety margin, not a separately derived law. The reasoning: (1) a Hann/half-Hann/Tukey taper widens the effective main lobe beyond the rectangular-window 1/T, so effective resolution is coarser than 1/T (roughly 1.5–2× for a Hann window); (2) at exactly 1/T you have a single bin describing the feature, so any narrow feature is unresolved and the magnitude is biased low; (3) empirically, a feature is only well captured when the window resolution is at least as fine as the feature's bandwidth. The best concrete evidence is CharlieLaub's controlled ARTA experiment (diyAudio): for a Q=20, ~200 Hz-wide peak, "The 2.5 ms (400 Hz) window is only partly resolving the peak… The 5 ms (200 Hz) window does a pretty good job." That is: when resolution equals the feature width (1/T at the feature bandwidth) you capture it; at 2× coarser you only "partly" capture it. So the 2–3× convention is defensible as prudence — it ensures the taper-widened effective bin is comfortably finer than typical features — but it is not backed by a published dB-error-vs-cycles table. No primary source quantifies the exact magnitude error at 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0 cycles; treat any such number as your own measurement rather than citing folklore.

Practical implication for your floor. Taking 2.5/T = 357 Hz at 7 ms is a sound, conservative choice. It correctly implies that anything you assert between ~143 Hz (1/T) and ~357 Hz (2.5/T) is a "grey zone" where the estimate exists but is taper-biased and low-resolution. 410 Hz is only just above this floor (2.87 cycles), so it inherits substantial grey-zone risk — see Q6.

Question 3 — Multi-gate convergence as a validation method

Is it named/formalized in audio tools? No. No loudspeaker measurement package (REW, ARTA, Acourate, Audiolense, Klippel) formalizes "re-window at several gates and keep only convergent features" into a per-feature confidence score. What exists:

* REW can overlay multiple IR windows and has a Multi-Time-Window (MTW) option whose "width is specified at octave centre frequencies" — one AV NIRVANA user aptly called it "a kind of variable frequency dependent windowing fully customizable." But this is a display/analysis convenience, not a robustness scorer.
* ARTA practice explicitly compares gated vs. ungated (open-window) responses to see where the room takes over, and its near/far splice workflow uses the overlap region as an implicit consistency check ("Over lap of the far field and near field can be good as you can see if your baffle assumptions are correct").
* Klippel NFS uses a genuine internal error metric — it monitors reflection consistency and reports field-separation quality — but that validates the holographic model, not a gate ladder.

Strong analogues in the wider literature (where your idea is grounded). Your approach is essentially a jackknife/bootstrap over the window parameter, which is exactly how the multitaper spectral-estimation community estimates uncertainty. Thomson's multitaper method computes the variance of a spectral estimate by jackknifing over orthogonal tapers: "the spectra… are approximately independent… and hence can be used to produce an estimate of variance," and the jackknife variance "always exceeds the true variance, so the estimates are conservative." Welch's method relies on consistency across overlapping segments. The formal justification for "trust convergent features" is precisely that features stable under resampling of the analysis window have low estimator variance, while features that swing with the window are dominated by leakage/reflection bias.

Assessment & recommendation. Your multi-gate convergence scorer is legitimate and, as far as the public record shows, novel in the loudspeaker-measurement context — you would be importing a well-established statistical idea (jackknife-over-window-parameter) that no audio tool has formalized. Keep it, and make it quantitative in the multitaper spirit — for each feature (peak/dip center, Q, level), compute the spread across your 3/5/7 ms ladder (and ideally add FDW cycle-count variants), and expose a per-feature variance/confidence. Treat a feature whose level varies by more than ~1–2 dB across the ladder as low-confidence and ineligible for narrow boost. This directly answers your 410 Hz case: if the +2.2 dB feature is not stable across 3/5/7 ms, it fails its own gate-stability test.

Question 4 — Getting truth below the gate floor

(a) Near-field measurement and near-field + port summing (Keele; D'Appolito)

The math. Keele's 1974 result (JAES 22(3):154–162) is that in the near field, at low frequencies where the diaphragm is a rigid piston, the on-axis near-field pressure is directly proportional to the far-field pressure and independent of the environment. For multiple circular radiators, the total near-field pressure is the complex (magnitude and phase) sum weighted by diameter:
p_tot = p₁·D₁ + p₂·D₂ + … + pₙ·Dₙ (D'Appolito Eq. 1, after Keele)
Equivalently, weight each measured pressure by √(area) of its radiator. For a woofer + port, the practical form is:
p_tot = p_woofer + p_port·(D_port/D_woofer)
D'Appolito's worked example: D_port/D_woofer = 5.5/13.8 = 0.398 = −8.0 dB, so the raw port measurement (which can look ~8 dB too high because the port is smaller) is scaled down by 20·log₁₀(D_port/D_woofer). For a rear/side radiator, add a further level correction for the difference in distance to the reference plane. The port is summed with its true measured phase (the woofer and port are out of phase below fB, which produces the vented roll-off — D'Appolito: "the woofer and port responses are out of phase below fB so the response results from a subtraction of two relatively large quantities").

Near-to-far / piston conversion. The near-field pressure scales to the equivalent 1 m far-field (half-space) level using the piston relation; Keele's development gives the far-field on-axis SPL from the near-field via the radiating radius a. The critical practical point (both Keele and D'Appolito) is that the near-field measurement yields the 2π half-space (infinite-baffle) response, so you must add back the baffle-step/diffraction correction (the 2π→4π transition, up to 6 dB of "spreading loss") before the near-field curve represents a real finite-baffle speaker. Klippel AN39 and Audioholics both note the diffraction model must be combined with near-field data prior to splicing.

Enumerated error sources (Keele via D'Appolito, and Klippel AN39):

* Upper frequency limit set by radiator size: near-field valid only for ka<1. D'Appolito's rule: fmax ≈ 4311/D (D in inches) for an infinite baffle; for the 7″/5″-effective example, fmax = 862 Hz. Klippel: fmax = 5475/a[cm] (a = effective radius), i.e., the ka=1 limit.
* Mic distance from the cone must be ≪ radius. Per D'Appolito (audioXpress, June 2012): "Keele shows that a microphone distance less than 0.11 times the diaphragm effective radius results in measurement errors of less than 1 dB… the microphone should be placed within 0.275″ of the driver dust cap" (for a ~6.5″ driver). Klippel: <11% of cone radius → <1 dB error.
* Cone breakup / non-uniform velocity invalidates the piston assumption above breakup. Per D'Appolito (audioXpress, June 2012): "At higher frequencies, where cone break up begins, pressure waves from various areas of the diaphragm may arrive at the microphone out-of-phase, causing near-field response cancellations that are not observed at normal listening distances."
* Port organ-pipe resonances and turbulence. Ports show resonances (D'Appolito's example: an "up tick in port response around 400 Hz… possibly caused by woofer back wave leaking out through the port"), and their valid upper limit is "much lower than that for a diaphragm of the same diameter." Drive at 2.83 V to avoid port nonlinearity corrupting the result.
* Crosstalk between closely spaced radiators. Port output at fB can be ~25 dB above woofer output; even 20 dB-down crosstalk shifts the apparent fB (D'Appolito shows an 8.9 Hz fB error from crosstalk). A large disagreement between fB from impedance vs. near-field is the tell-tale.
* Multiple ports/passive radiators: measure each and sum complex with area weighting; for a passive radiator place the mic between the two radiators (Klippel's cancellation-point method). Small's Microphone-in-Box (MIB) alternative needs only one in-box pressure measurement (no phase), is often more accurate below fB, but breaks down above the frequency where the largest internal dimension = ⅛ wavelength.
* Half-space loading, not the real baffle — restated because it is the most common splice error: near-field gives 2π; you must add diffraction.

(b) Ground-plane measurement

Place the mic on a large hard reflective plane and the source on/near the same plane; the mirror image doubles the pressure, giving +6 dB and a reflection that is coincident (in-phase, no comb filtering up to a high frequency). To recover the equivalent free-field 1 m level, measure at 2 m (double the intended distance) so the 6 dB distance loss cancels the 6 dB ground gain (Struck & Temme JAES 1994; Gander JAES 1982). Practical rules (Audio Precision app note, ProSoundWeb, Audioholics, prosoundtraining):

* Tilt/aim the source at the on-ground mic (a single woofer can be tilted; a multi-driver line should be parallel to the plane with drivers aimed at the mic).
* The plane must be large relative to the lowest wavelength and free of other reflectors; outdoors on tarmac/concrete is common. To get reflections 20 dB down for an elevated (non-ground-plane) setup you'd need 5–10 m of height — impractical, which is exactly why ground-plane is used.
* Limits: finite plane size sets a low-frequency edge-diffraction limit; the source's vertical extent limits high-frequency validity (the image must remain coincident); ambient noise/wind outdoors.

(c) Klippel Near-Field Scanner (NFS)

The NFS moves a mic over a spherical/cylindrical (double-layer) scanning surface in the near field and, per Klippel, "The sound field generated by the source is reconstructed by a weighted sum of spherical harmonics and Hankel functions which are solutions of the wave equation," using "field separation techniques to extract the direct sound and to reduce room reflections." Because it captures a double shell, it distinguishes the outgoing (direct) wave from the incoming (room-reflected) wave, "thereby getting true anechoic data down to very low frequencies in an ordinary room." Klippel notes N=0 (monopole) "almost completely describes the sound power between 20 and 200 Hz." Per Misco, "The Near Field Scanner system costs approximately $100,000 — significantly less expensive than building a new anechoic chamber" (chamber construction "often exceeding half a million dollars"). Practical limits (Klippel): the "device under test (< 500 kg) does not move during the scanning process," and it must be a compact source (the expansion assumes compactness); the model degrades if the room's reflections are not time-invariant during the scan; and it characterizes the speaker, not the speaker-in-your-room.

When practitioners choose each, and how to splice. Near-field is the default for enclosed compact speakers where 1 m is in the far field of the driver; ground-plane for larger systems/subs and for validating sensitivity; NFS when full anechoic directivity is needed. Splicing (Klippel AN39, D'Appolito, Audioholics):

* Choose the splice/blend frequency in the overlap where the gated far-field is valid (above ~1/gate) and the near-field is still valid (below ka=1) — typically 200–500 Hz. Klippel default: 400–500 Hz. "There is no good theory for picking the merge point" (D'Appolito) — pick where the curves overlap best.
* Level-match by shifting the near-field (2π, diffraction-added) level to meet the measured far-field sensitivity in the overlap band (the far-field is the sensitivity reference). Klippel: minimize RMS error in a one-octave band around the splice.
* Phase continuity: sum near-field components complex, and if you need a continuous complex response, align delays so phase is continuous at the splice.
* What goes wrong: (1) forgetting to add baffle step, so the spliced LF is 3–6 dB too high (the classic 2π/4π error); (2) splicing where the gated far-field is already invalid (below the true 1/T or 2.5/T floor); (3) crosstalk/scaling errors in the port sum; (4) a real driver feature near the splice (like D'Appolito's 400 Hz port artifact) being mistaken for a splice mismatch, or vice-versa. Klippel offers an LPM "second opinion" (computing the 1 m SPL from Thiele-Small parameters and effective Sd) to cross-check the near-field level.

Question 5 — Deciding whether a feature is minimum-phase (hence EQ-correctable)

The established method: excess group delay from the Hilbert-derived minimum phase. Generate the minimum-phase response corresponding to the measured magnitude (Hilbert/Bode relation), subtract measured phase to get excess phase, and take its slope: excess group delay. REW's help states it plainly: "Anywhere the excess group delay plot is flat is a minimum phase region of the response." Acourate and others implement the same. The theory: a minimum-phase system has all zeros inside the unit circle (left half-plane), the lowest possible group delay for its magnitude, and is invertible; a non-minimum-phase response has zeros outside the unit circle (nulls/cancellations) that no EQ can undo.

When room features are / are not minimum-phase (the key literature):

* Neely & Allen (JASA 1979, 66(1):165–169) — the seminal result. Per the MDPI 2018 review citing them: "if the reflectivity of the wall is low (below 36%) the RTF is minimum-phase and thus invertible… with larger wall reflection coefficients, as those of typical rooms (in the range 70%–90%), the RIR is non-minimum-phase." The original paper notes the threshold "was lowered" (to about 33%) when source and receiver were more separated. Even their successful minimum-phase inversion left an audible artifact ("a tone, much like a bell chime").
* REW/Mulcahy's own simulation demonstrates the mechanism: individual axial modes are each perfectly minimum-phase, but summing them is not — "adding the responses of minimum phase systems gives a result which is typically not minimum phase," because wherever two contributions are equal in magnitude and opposite in phase, their sum is zero and "that zero level cannot be restored… by any amount of EQ." So even at low frequencies a room can be locally non-minimum-phase (his example: 44–56 Hz and 70–120 Hz). He adds the operational rule: "Narrow bandwidth EQ adjustments should not be used outside the modal range."
* Toole (Sound Reproduction): isolated low-frequency resonances behave as minimum-phase, so correcting amplitude also corrects phase — the basis for modal EQ.
* Karjalainen, Mäkivirta, Antsalo, Välimäki (Modal Equalization of Loudspeaker-Room Responses at Low Frequencies, JAES 2003; Estimation of Modal Decay Parameters from Noisy Response Measurements, JAES 2002; Frequency-Zooming ARMA Modeling, JAES 2002; "About Room Response Equalization and Dereverberation," WASPAA 2005): formalize modeling modes as pole-zero systems and equalizing the minimum-phase/modal part while treating the rest as mixed-phase. Mourjopoulos and Bharitkar & Kyriakakis extend to multipoint and cepstral minimum-phase extraction. Consensus: correct the minimum-phase (modal, peak) part; do not attempt to invert the mixed-phase cancellation part.

Known pitfalls (all directly relevant to your pipeline):

* The windowing itself corrupts excess group delay. REW: the minimum-phase response "is derived from the frequency (magnitude) response… which in turn is affected by the IR window settings," and "it is best to make full range measurements… a good result relies on measuring beyond the bandwidth of the system." A short gate biases the min-phase reconstruction — so your min-phase test at 410 Hz will itself be contaminated by the 7 ms gate.
* Time-of-flight offset produces a linear phase slope that looks like huge group delay; remove t=0 delay first (REW's "Offset t=0" / min-phase delay estimation).
* Insufficient bandwidth for a valid Hilbert transform (especially near Nyquist and near the low-frequency edge of a gated measurement).
* Noise corrupts the phase and hence excess group delay.
* A dip that is direct/reflected cancellation is not minimum-phase — the single most important pitfall for your case. REW: "a reflection that is exactly the same amplitude as the direct signal… combine[s to] zero amplitude… That zero level cannot be restored… by any amount of EQ." Boosting it is futile (the boost lifts direct and reflected equally, they still cancel) or harmful (wasted excursion/headroom).

Question 6 — When is a boost near the gate floor supportable?

The "cut don't boost" convention and its dissenters. The mainstream conservative position (WiiM/ASR practitioners, Toole-influenced): default the boost range to 0 dB and allow cuts, because boosting "decreases the overall max volume level… will cause strong resonances elsewhere in the room at the original dip frequency… saves amplifier headroom and avoids temporal ringing." Cutting everything except a dip is mathematically equivalent to boosting the dip with reduced pre-gain, so the asymmetry is really about headroom, not correctability. Dissent: broad, shallow boosts to counter a genuinely minimum-phase roll-off (extending a sealed sub's LF, or a broad baffle-step shelf) are widely accepted and effective; the objection is specifically to narrow boosts of cancellation dips. Even dissenters agree (REW "Why Can't I Fix All my Acoustic Problems with EQ?"): "the best we can do is deal with the broad, shallow dips and work on the peaks"; narrow deep dips are position-sensitive, non-minimum-phase, and headroom-hungry.

SBIR — why a 410 Hz dip at 1 m is a prime suspect. Speaker-boundary interference response is destructive interference between direct sound and a boundary reflection when the path-length difference is a half wavelength: f_dip ≈ 172/Δpath (m) for the first null (Δpath = reflected − direct path). A dip at 410 Hz implies Δpath ≈ 0.42 m — entirely plausible for a floor or nearby-boundary bounce with a mic at 1 m in a small room. The unanimous literature verdict (GIK, Acoustic Frontiers, Arqen, Arendal): SBIR nulls cannot be fixed with EQ — "equalization cannot recover energy lost to destructive interference… boosting the cancellation frequency will also amplify the reflection causing the interference." They are addressed by placement and treatment, not EQ. Identify SBIR by: (1) it moves with mic/speaker position; (2) it appears in the min-phase test as a non-flat excess-group-delay spike; (3) it computes to a plausible boundary distance; (4) it deepens or fills depending on gate/FDW (the diagnostic asymmetry from Q1).

Your specific case: +2.2 dB at 410 Hz, 7 ms gate (≈2.87 cycles), floor set at 357 Hz. Verdict: marginal-to-unsupportable as justified, and most likely unsupportable. Reasons:

1. 410 Hz is only 2.87 cycles in your gate — above your 2.5/T floor (357 Hz) but below the conservative 3/T (429 Hz) line, squarely in the taper-biased grey zone where the magnitude estimate has low resolution and is sensitive to the exact window.
2. At 1 m in a small room, 410 Hz is in the transition region and a floor/ceiling-bounce SBIR null is the single most plausible physical cause of a dip there — and SBIR dips must not be EQ'd.
3. A narrow boost is the worst case for both headroom and non-minimum-phase futility.

What the pipeline should require before applying such a boost (staged test):

* Gate-stability test (you already have this): is the 410 Hz feature stable in center, depth, and Q across 3/5/7 ms? If it swings, reject. This is your multitaper-jackknife criterion from Q3.
* Min-phase test: generate min phase from a full-range (ungated or long-window) measurement and check excess group delay is flat at 410 Hz. A spike ⇒ non-minimum-phase ⇒ do not boost. (The gate corrupts this — use the longest clean window you can.)
* Corroborate below the floor: take a near-field (woofer, and port if vented, summed with area weighting and baffle step added) and/or a ground-plane measurement. If the anechoic-equivalent response is smooth through 410 Hz, the in-room dip is room-caused (SBIR/modal cancellation), not a speaker feature — do not boost.
* Spatial averaging: measure at several mic positions. If the dip moves in frequency or fills in, it is interference — do not boost. If it is stable across space and minimum-phase and present in the anechoic-equivalent, only then is a modest, broad boost defensible.

If, after all four, the feature survives, apply a broad (low-Q) boost of ≤ the amount your headroom allows, not a narrow 2.2 dB spike — and re-verify it does not create a null elsewhere.

Recommendations (staged, with thresholds)

Stage 1 — Fix the 410 Hz decision now. Treat the current +2.2 dB @ 410 Hz filter as unproven and disable it pending corroboration. Run the four-part test above. Threshold to keep it: feature stable within ±1 dB across the 3/5/7 ms ladder AND flat excess group delay at 410 Hz (from a long-window measurement) AND present in a near-field or ground-plane anechoic-equivalent AND spatially stable across ≥3 mic positions. If any one fails, drop the boost and (if the dip is audible) address it by placement/treatment. Given the physics, expect it to fail on the SBIR/min-phase criterion.

Stage 2 — Formalize multi-gate convergence as a first-class uncertainty estimator. You are on solid ground; no audio tool does this. Implement it as a jackknife-over-window: for each candidate feature, compute the across-gate (and across-FDW-cycle-count) variance of center frequency, Q, and level; expose a per-feature confidence. Gate eligibility for narrow boosts on low variance. Benchmark: a feature whose level spread exceeds ~2 dB across gates is boost-ineligible.

Stage 3 — Adopt "compute both, disagreement is information" for the FDW question. Produce fixed-gate, FDW (5 and 15 cycles), and variable/psychoacoustic-smoothed views. Use agreement across all as your correctability gate; use FDW-vs-fixed disagreement (dip fills in under FDW) as a positive SBIR/reflection detector. Do not derive EQ targets directly from an FDW'd magnitude below ~300 Hz. Revisit if a peer-reviewed comparison appears (none exists today).

Stage 4 — Build the sub-floor truth path. Add a near-field capture routine (mic <11% of cone radius; fmax = 4311/D_in or 5475/a_cm; complex area-weighted woofer+port sum; add modeled baffle step; splice 200–500 Hz by RMS-minimizing level-match against the gated far-field sensitivity). Add a ground-plane option (2 m for equivalent 1 m free-field, +6 dB accounted). This gives you real data below 357 Hz so you stop guessing in the grey zone. Threshold for trusting a spliced LF feature: near-field and gated far-field agree within ~1 dB across a one-octave overlap.

Stage 5 — Keep the min-phase gate on every boost, everywhere. Never boost a feature whose excess group delay is not flat over its band, and never boost outside the modal range with narrow filters.

Caveats

* The FDW-vs-fixed-gate question is genuinely unresolved. There is no peer-reviewed, listening-test-validated comparison of the two as EQ-target generators. My recommendation ("compute both") is a risk-management stance, not a proof that one is superior.
* The 2.5/T–3/T floor is a defensible convention, not a quantified law. Primary sources state fmin = 1/T; the 2–3× margin is engineering prudence and, in the diyAudio experiment, corresponds to the empirical fact that features narrower than the window resolution are only partly captured. No source found tabulates the dB error at 2/2.5/3 cycles — if you need that number, measure it in your own pipeline rather than citing folklore.
* Do not attribute an "FDW creates artifacts / never use FDW for EQ" position to Mulcahy. His documented stance is narrower: FDW and variable smoothing are "not equivalent," plain smoothing "provides better EQ targets" in his tests, smoothing is recommended for EQ above ~200 Hz, and FDW and smoothing "can be combined." He built EQ-target-match to work with FDW'd responses.
* Several forum sources (diyAudio, HTS, AVS, WiiM) are practitioner statements, not peer-reviewed; I have flagged where a claim rests on a forum post vs. a primary paper or tool-author statement. The Keele 1974 and Neely & Allen 1979 papers were accessible at abstract level plus secondary quotation; the quantitative near-field math is taken from D'Appolito's CLIO/audioXpress articles (which restate Keele's method) and Klippel AN39, both primary-adjacent.
* hometheatershack.com blocked direct fetch (HTTP 402); Mulcahy's verbatim quotes from that thread were recovered from consistent search-cache extracts of the exact thread URLs and cross-checked against his materially identical statements on AV NIRVANA (avnirvana.com) and roomeqwizard.com.
* Your case analysis assumes the 410 Hz feature is a dip (cancellation). If it is instead a narrow peak you are boosting the shoulder of, the min-phase logic flips (peaks are usually correctable) — but the Stage 1 tests still apply, and a narrow boost outside the modal range remains ill-advised per Mulcahy's rule.
