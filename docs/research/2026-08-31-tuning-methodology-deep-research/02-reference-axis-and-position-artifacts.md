# Reference Axis, Horn Mouth-Edge Diffraction, and EQ Target Selection: A Technical Investigation for a 2-Way Active Horn System

## TL;DR
- **The four suspect filters (2323, 3952, 4450, 7310 Hz; Q 1.5–2.6; up to −6 dB) should be provisionally removed or sharply reduced pending a persistence-across-angle test.** Their irregular spacing, moderate Q, and the fact that they were fit to a single gated 0° measurement are the classic signature of on-axis interference artifacts (mouth-edge diffraction and/or a windowing/reflection residual), which the published evidence says should NOT be equalized because they do not represent the broadband sound field the ear integrates.
- **Measuring a horn/waveguide on a declared axis other than 0° is legitimate and standards-sanctioned** — IEC 60268-5 explicitly makes the reference axis a *manufacturer-declared* quantity, not necessarily the geometric axis — but the specific "listen ~10–22° off the horn axis" habit is Geddes-school design practice/convention, not a written standard requirement. CTA-2034 and AES56 assume a nominal on-axis reference but derive their key curves (Listening Window, PIR) from *spatial averages* precisely so that single-axis interference artifacts are suppressed.
- **The correct EQ target for this system is a spatially-averaged curve (Listening Window / PIR-style), not the 0° on-axis curve.** The dominant, evidence-based failure mode of single-mic auto-EQ is that it "corrects" narrow-angle interference dips that are inaudible or benign, injecting real broadband peaks in exchange — and dips are far less audible than peaks (Bücklein 1981; Toole & Olive 1988).

## Key Findings

**On reference axis (Q1).** IEC 60268-5 defines the reference axis, reference point, and reference plane as *rated conditions* that "shall be stated by the manufacturer" and "cannot be measured." The standard notes only that *for symmetrical structures* the reference axis is "usually perpendicular to the radiating surface." There is no requirement that it be the geometric axis; declaring an off-geometric-axis reference is standard-compliant. CTA-2034-A and AES56 both work from a nominal on-axis "reference angle" but immediately spatially-average around it. The "listen ~10–22° off the horn axis" practice is specifically the Geddes waveguide school (design/listening axis ~22.5°) and is convention/design philosophy, not a standard.

**On mouth-edge diffraction signatures (Q2).** Geddes' aperture-diffraction limit (JAES 41(4), 1993, Formula #8), kR > 15/sin²(θ), states that above that limit "the frequency response and the directivity will be strongly affected by the mouth diffraction." BEM/ABEC simulation evidence (mabat/ATH) shows that an *ideal source* driving a waveguide with a perfect mouth termination produces a dead-smooth response, and that any ripple is *interference* from the mouth edge — visible primarily on-axis and reduced/relocated off-axis, and curable by rolling the mouth edge past ~180°. This is the crux discriminator: **mouth-edge diffraction ripple is angle-dependent (interference-like); throat/driver and mouth-reflection resonances of the primary spherical wave are angle-independent and minimum-phase (legitimately EQ-able); HOMs are angle-dependent AND non-minimum-phase (not EQ-able).**

**On automated detection (Q3).** The principled, published method is *field/mode separation and persistence-across-angle*. The Klippel NFS uses spherical-harmonic/Hankel expansion with "Direct Sound Separation" to isolate the true radiated field. Harman's preference model (Olive 2004; US 8,311,232) computes its narrow-band-deviation (NBD) and smoothness (SM) metrics on *spatially-averaged* curves specifically because, in the patent's own words, "spatial averaging may be used for all the curves (except the on-axis curves, if provided) to remove interference and diffraction effects from the measurements." VituixCAD implements power/DI-weighted and listening-window/in-room-weighted optimization for the same reason. The operational criterion for "EQ-able" throughout the literature is position/angle-independence + minimum-phase behavior (Toole).

**On direct-radiator equivalents (Q4).** Baffle-edge diffraction is broadly similar across angle and minimum-phase — it is the textbook example of a legitimately EQ-able (or better, geometry-fixable via Olson-style chamfers) response feature. Cone/diaphragm breakup radiates differently at different angles and is frequently non-minimum-phase, and is the textbook example of something that should be crossed away from or mechanically damped rather than EQ'd flat on one axis.

**On fitting targets and spatial averaging (Q5).** CTA-2034 LW = average of 9 responses in the ±10° vertical / ±30° horizontal window; PIR = 12% LW + 44% early reflections + 44% sound power. The audibility literature (Bücklein 1981; Toole & Olive 1988) establishes that dips are much less audible than peaks and that filling a narrow dip with boost is the worst move — you spend real broadband energy to correct an artifact that may not have been audible.

## Details

### Q1 — Reference axis for horns and waveguides

**What the standards actually say.**

*IEC 60268-5 (Sound system equipment – Part 5: Loudspeakers, ed. 3.1, 2007).* Clause 15 covers "Reference plane, reference point and reference axis." These are *rated conditions* (Clause 3.2.1): "values (known as 'rated conditions') shall be taken from the manufacturer's specification. These values themselves are not subject to measurement." The explicit list of manufacturer-declared quantities includes "reference plane; reference point; reference axis." A NOTE adds: "Strictly speaking, these terms should include the word 'rated' … because they are specified by the manufacturer and cannot be measured." For the axis itself: "For symmetrical structures, the reference axis is usually perpendicular to the radiating surface." **[STANDARD] The reference axis is chosen and declared by the manufacturer; the perpendicular/geometric axis is described as the *usual* case, not a requirement.** This is the single most important standards fact for the user: declaring a non-geometric reference axis is fully compliant, and IEC 60268-5 (Clause 21) says the frequency response "shall be specified as measured … at a stated position with respect to the reference axis."

*AES56-2008 (r2019) (Sound source modeling – Loudspeaker polar radiation measurements).* Describes how polar data "shall be made and documented," using spherical coordinates (θ, ρ) relative to a reference axis under free-field conditions, for use in room-acoustic/sound-system prediction. **[STANDARD] AES56 standardizes the *acquisition and documentation* of the full polar set; it does not mandate that EQ or "the" response be taken on the geometric axis** — it presupposes you capture the whole balloon.

*ANSI/CTA-2034-A (Standard Method of Measurement for In-Home Loudspeakers, "spinorama," R-2020).* Data taken every 10° over horizontal and vertical orbits. The On-Axis (0°) response is "the universal starting point." The **Listening Window is a spatial average of the nine amplitude responses in the ±10° vertical and ±30° horizontal angular range.** **[STANDARD]** The standard's own framing treats 0° as a starting reference but derives its headline curves from spatial averages, structurally down-weighting single-axis artifacts.

**What manufacturers/designers actually do.**
- **Geddes school (GedLee Summa/Abbey; R-OSSE lineage):** design and listen ~22.5° off geometric axis. Geddes, verbatim (diyAudio "Geddes on Waveguides"): "In my deisgns I pretty mush ignore the axial response seeking to get the best 22.5 degree response with smooth and flat polar/power response. Typically the axial response is not ideal in this scenario." **[CONVENTION / documented design philosophy]** — this is exactly the school the R-OSSE waveguide the user is building descends from. The rationale is that the on-axis response of an OS/waveguide "looks bad if normalized to the axial response because of the mouth diffraction," so an off-axis listening axis avoids the on-axis interference zone (bmc0/AllenB, diyAudio).
- **Constant-directivity pro horns (Peavey, JBL, and the general CD-horn tradition):** the CD horn "no longer measures flat directly on axis without its needed signal processing … designed to be the reciprocal or mirror image of the horn/driver high frequency roll off." Peavey's published account confirms that CD horns are intentionally EQ'd with an HF boost (≈ +6 dB/oct above the diffraction frequency) — an *on-axis* correction that is legitimate because it corrects a broadband directivity-driven roll-off, not an interference ripple. **[MEASUREMENT-supported industry practice]**

**Verdict on Q1:** Off-axis reference-axis declaration is standard-sanctioned (IEC 60268-5) and, for waveguides specifically, is an evidence-motivated design habit of the Geddes/R-OSSE school. It is *not* a written standard requirement that horns be measured at 10°. The "~10° off-axis" number in particular is closer to folklore/rule-of-thumb; the Geddes ~22.5° figure is a documented design axis with a stated physical rationale.

### Q2 — Horn mouth-edge diffraction signatures and whether the four filters match

**Mechanism and frequency signature.** The finite mouth reflects the wave when the wavelength is large relative to the mouth; the reflected/re-radiated wave interferes with the direct wave, producing ripple. The classic "infinite mouth" rule is that a mouth circumference ≈ wavelength of the lowest frequency is "close enough to infinite"; below that "the response will have dips and peaks" (community/standard rule-of-thumb). Patent literature (US4324313) states the physics cleanly: "the reflecting wave has an important influence in the low range of sounds while the diffraction wave [dominates] the high range" — i.e., mouth *reflection* ripple is a low-frequency-end effect, while edge *diffraction* interference extends higher. Ripple spacing in frequency follows a round-trip standing-wave relation, Δf ≈ c/(2L), with L the effective throat-to-mouth path. **[MEASUREMENT/theory-supported for the mechanism; the Kolbrek & Dunker (2019) "mouth circumference/half-wavelength ↔ ripple spacing" specific statement could not be verified from primary text and is flagged UNVERIFIED.]**

**Angle-dependence — the decisive point.** BEM/ABEC simulation (mabat/ATH) shows:
- With an *ideal source*, "the frequency response could be absolutely smooth with no wiggle whatsoever. Any wiggle you see is result of some interference … a reflection or diffraction related backwave that emits when sound bends (diffracts) around a corner."
- On-axis ripple "seems to be edge diffraction on a freestanding waveguide … Rolling over the mouth edge bit more than 180 degrees would get rid of it."
- Mouth diffraction is *most visible on-axis* and when normalizing polars to 0°; "smoother looking waveguide polars tend to change their directivity continuously, which is a symptom of distributed diffraction." **[SIMULATION EVIDENCE + community]**

Geddes' own theory gives the physical taxonomy (Geddes verbatim + *Audio Transducers* Ch. 6, patents US7708112B2):
- **Primary-wave resonances** (throat/driver, and mouth reflection of the fundamental spherical wave): "It is spherical in all cases … its effect will be seen to be the SAME AT ALL RADIATED FIELD POINTS and it can in fact be exactly Eq'd by electronic means … indistinguishable from a diaphragm resonance." → **angle-independent, minimum-phase, EQ-able.**
- **HOMs (higher-order modes)** from diffraction/slope/area discontinuities: "propagate by reflecting off of the horn walls," non-spherical, radiate differently at different angles, and are **non-minimum-phase → not EQ-able.**

**Do the four filters match a mouth-diffraction signature?** The candidate mechanisms for narrow-to-moderate-Q features at 2323/3952/4450/7310 Hz:

| Mechanism | Expected angle behavior | Min-phase? | EQ verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mouth-edge diffraction ripple | Strong on-axis, washes/shifts off-axis | mixed/interference | Do NOT EQ on one axis |
| Mouth reflection of primary wave | Same at all angles | yes | EQ-able |
| Throat discontinuity / driver cavity resonance (phase-plug annular modes) | Same at all angles (source property) | yes | EQ-able |
| Diaphragm breakup | Varies with angle | often no | Cross away / damp, don't EQ flat |
| Waveguide HOMs | Varies with angle | no | Not EQ-able; damp (foam) |
| Cabinet baffle diffraction | Broadly similar across angle | yes | EQ-able (better: geometry) |
| Gating/windowing residual or surviving room reflection | Appears/shifts with mic position & gate; often absent in true anechoic/NFS data | artifact | Do NOT EQ |

The four suspect frequencies span 2.3–7.3 kHz with irregular spacing and moderate Q (1.5–2.6) — this is *consistent with* mouth-edge diffraction interference and/or a gating residual, and *distinguishable from* a phase-plug/driver resonance (which would persist identically at all angles) only by the persistence-across-angle test. The B&C DE250 is a 1" (25 mm throat) polyimide-diaphragm driver with a concentric-slot phase plug (≈12–13° included exit-tube angle); annular-channel compression drivers do have documented cavity/annular modes that can notch the response (Voishvillo, AES 141, preprint 9618; Dodd et al., JAES 57(10), 2009), but those are *source* properties that appear at *all* angles. That is exactly why the ±7/±22° family is diagnostic.

### Q3 — Automated detection of axis-local artifacts

- **Field/mode separation (Klippel NFS):** spherical-harmonic + Hankel-function expansion with patented Direct Sound Separation isolates the outgoing (direct) field from reflections; the order N is raised with frequency (e.g., N=5 below 500 Hz up to N=14 above 2 kHz) and a fitting-error metric E(f) flags where the model fails (below −20 dB = 1% error over most of the band). The user's DIY cylindrical scanner reproduces exactly this: two closely-spaced cylindrical surfaces enable field separation, so a true anechoic dataset can reveal whether a "dip" is a real radiated feature or a gating/room residual. **[PRIMARY, Klippel documentation]**
- **Spatially-averaged / variance-weighted targets (Harman/Olive; VituixCAD):** Olive's preference model (US 8,311,232; US20050195982A1; AES 2004) computes NBD and SM on spatially-averaged curves and states plainly (verbatim, US 8,311,232 B2): "spatial averaging may be used for all the curves (except the on-axis curves, if provided) to remove interference and diffraction effects from the measurements." The model's regression uses, e.g., Pref = 12.69 − 2.49·NBD_ON − 2.99·NBD_PIR − 4.31·LFX + 2.32·SM_PIR — three of four terms operate on the spatially-averaged PIR or on extension, not the raw axis. VituixCAD's documented practice: optimize to listening-window (≈ −0.2 dB/oct) and in-room (≈ −0.8 to −0.9 dB/oct) targets with power/DI weighting, not to a single axis. **[PRIMARY + documented practitioner practice]** These are the deployed embodiments of "decline to correct axis-local features."
- **The EQ-ability criterion (Toole):** the operational rule in the literature is that a feature is correctable by EQ if it is (i) position/angle-independent and (ii) minimum-phase — because then correcting amplitude also corrects phase and the transient/ringing. Toole: room resonances at low frequencies "behave as 'minimum phase' phenomena, and so, if the amplitude vs frequency characteristic is corrected, so also will the phase … the ringing, or overhang, must be eliminated." The corollary is that a narrow feature that changes with angle fails test (i) and must not be single-axis EQ'd. **[Toole, published position; the low-frequency minimum-phase claim is measurement-supported, the extension to narrow HF interference is a reasoned/asserted position]**

### Q4 — Direct-radiator equivalents

- **Baffle-edge diffraction:** Olson's classic enclosure-shape experiments (12 cabinet forms; sphere smoothest; 45° chamfers on a rectangular box nearly matched the sphere) established that edge diffraction produces re-radiation delayed and ~180° shifted, causing ripple whose *character is broadly similar across the forward hemisphere*. Vanderkooy's GTD-based model (secondary edge point-sources at the baffle edge) reproduces this. Because it is broadly angle-similar and minimum-phase, baffle diffraction is the textbook *legitimately EQ-able* feature — though designers (Olson, and modern narrow-baffle/chamfer practice) prefer to fix it with geometry. ESP/Elliott summarizes the standard practical view: baffle-step and diffraction ripple "are typically dealt with by utilizing equalization in the crossover network." **[MEASUREMENT-supported (Olson, Vanderkooy); EQ-ability is the documented consensus]**
- **Cone/diaphragm breakup:** radiates differently at different angles and is frequently non-minimum-phase; the documented practice (D'Appolito, "Testing Loudspeakers"; Zaph/Krutke published measurement sets; VituixCAD guidance) is to cross away from breakup or mechanically damp it, not to EQ a single-axis breakup peak flat, because doing so mis-corrects every other angle and cannot fix the ringing. **[MEASUREMENT-supported + documented practice]**

This asymmetry is the direct-radiator analog of the horn case: baffle diffraction ≈ mouth reflection of the primary wave (EQ-able); breakup ≈ HOMs (not EQ-able on one axis).

### Q5 — Fitting targets, spatial averaging, and audibility

- **CTA-2034 definitions:** LW = spatial average of the 9 responses in ±10° V / ±30° H. Early Reflections = estimate of single-bounce first reflections in a typical room. Sound Power = all directions. **PIR ("room curve"), verbatim: "a usefully accurate Predicted In-Room (PIR) amplitude response, also known as a 'room curve' is obtained by a weighted average consisting of 12 % listening window, 44 % early reflections and 44 % sound power."** **[STANDARD]**
- **When to use LW/PIR instead of on-axis:** for a controlled-directivity speaker whose on-axis curve carries interference artifacts, the LW (which averages them down) or a directivity-appropriate in-room slope is the correct EQ target. VituixCAD practice above is the concrete recipe. **[Documented practice; consistent with the standard's own construction]**
- **Harman spatial-averaging practice for in-room:** Toole/Welti multi-position spatial averaging (a cluster of positions around the listening area, and Welti's multi-sub work for LF) is the documented method; the general principle is that a single mic point is not representative. **[Toole, Welti — published]**
- **Documented failure mode of single-mic EQ:** correcting a narrow dip that exists only at one point/angle injects a broadband error everywhere else; and dips are far less audible than peaks. Bücklein (1981, JAES 29(3):126–131), verbatim: "Peaks in the frequency response are far more audible than equivalent valleys or dips. Peaks are detected even when valleys of the same size are not perceptible." Toole & Olive (1988, JAES 36(3):122–142, "The Modification of Timbre by Resonances") quantify resonance detection thresholds as a function of frequency, Q, amplitude, time delay, program material, loudspeaker directivity and reverberation — with the important result that *low-Q (broad) resonances are detected at lower amplitude than high-Q (narrow) ones*, and a Q≈1 5-kHz resonance is detectable near 0.25 dB with pink noise under ideal conditions. **The combined implication: filling a narrow interference dip with correction (which for a dip means net boost to fill it) is spending audible broadband energy to chase an inaudible or benign feature — and the preference model's NBD term penalizes exactly the extra bumpiness this introduces.** **[MEASUREMENT-supported audibility science; the specific "correcting narrow on-axis dips degrades perceived result" is a reasoned inference from Bücklein + Toole/Olive + the Olive model, not a single dedicated experiment]**

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Immediate (use the ±7°/±22° family you already have).**
1. Overlay the tweeter response at 0°, ±7°, and ±22° *without* the four suspect filters, lightly smoothed (1/12–1/6 oct). For each suspect frequency, classify:
   - **If the dip is present at 0° but fills in or shifts in frequency by ±7°/±22°** → it is an on-axis interference artifact (mouth-edge diffraction or a gating residual). **Remove the filter.** In the competing-hypotheses table, this is the mouth-diffraction/windowing signature.
   - **If the dip sits at the same frequency and similar depth at 0°, ±7°, and ±22°** → it is a source/broadband feature (driver cavity/phase-plug mode, primary-wave resonance, or diaphragm breakup). **It is a candidate for EQ**, but confirm minimum-phase (below) and prefer a gentle correction fit to the LW, not the 0° curve.
2. Re-derive the EQ target as the **average of 0°/±7°/±22°** (a poor-man's Listening Window) rather than 0° alone, and re-fit. Filters that vanish under this averaging were correcting artifacts.

**Stage 2 — Confirm mechanism.**
3. Test whether each feature is a gating/windowing artifact: vary the gate length and mic distance. A true acoustic feature is stable; a windowing residual or surviving reflection moves or changes depth. (Your NFS-style rig removes this ambiguity entirely.)
4. Check minimum-phase: compare measured excess-phase / compute the Hilbert-derived minimum-phase response in REW. A feature that is minimum-phase and angle-stable is EQ-able; one that is non-minimum-phase or angle-varying is not.

**Stage 3 — Full polar scan (your DIY NFS).**
5. Run the field-separated full-sphere scan. Compute the CTA-2034 set (VituixCAD ingests the data). Fit EQ to the **Listening Window** (target ≈ −0.2 dB/oct) and check the **PIR/in-room** slope (≈ −0.8 to −0.9 dB/oct), with power/DI weighting. Do not fit to 0°.
6. For any feature that survives as a genuine, angle-independent, minimum-phase driver resonance (plausibly the DE250 phase-plug/cavity modes in the upper kHz), apply the *minimum* correction that flattens the LW — not the depth needed to flatten 0°.

**Benchmarks that change the decision.**
- A suspect feature that is **present-and-stable within ±0.5 dB across 0°/±7°/±22°** → keep a gentle, LW-fit filter.
- A feature that **changes by > ~2 dB or shifts frequency across ±22°** → remove; it is axis-local.
- Anything that **disappears when the gate is lengthened or in the NFS field-separated data** → remove; it was a measurement artifact.
- If the LR4 acoustic crossover at 2.5 kHz is verified, treat the 2323 Hz filter with particular suspicion: it sits right at the crossover skirt where summation/interference and gating errors concentrate.

**What the ±7/±22° data look like in each competing hypothesis (quick reference):**
- *Mouth-edge diffraction:* dip deepest at 0°, progressively fills and/or migrates in frequency by ±22°; directivity stays smooth (consistent with your "smooth to 8 kHz" observation).
- *Gating/reflection residual:* dip position/depth changes with gate length and distance, not cleanly with angle; often absent in field-separated NFS data.
- *Driver phase-plug/cavity mode or primary-wave resonance:* dip nearly identical at 0°/±7°/±22° (source property), minimum-phase — the only class that justifies keeping a (gentle, LW-fit) filter.
- *Diaphragm breakup / HOM:* feature present but *changes shape/level with angle* and tends non-minimum-phase — do not EQ; address by crossover choice or damping.

## Caveats
- **Convention-vs-evidence, restated:** The manufacturer-declared reference axis is written into IEC 60268-5 (STANDARD). The specific 10°/22.5° off-axis waveguide habit is Geddes-school design convention with a stated physical rationale (CONVENTION, physically motivated). The angle-dependence of mouth-edge diffraction and the HOM-vs-primary-resonance minimum-phase taxonomy are Geddes' theory plus BEM simulation (SIMULATION + THEORY; not a codified standard). The audibility asymmetry of peaks vs dips is peer-reviewed measurement (Bücklein; Toole & Olive — EVIDENCE). The claim that "correcting narrow on-axis dips degrades the perceived result" is a well-supported inference, not a single dedicated listening test.
- **Unverified item:** the Kolbrek & Dunker (2019, "High-Quality Horn Loudspeaker Systems") specific relationship between mouth circumference/half-wavelength and ripple spacing could not be confirmed from primary text; the underlying physics is corroborated by patent (US4324313) and standard rule-of-thumb literature.
- **Source-quality note:** several mechanism-level statements rest on diyAudio posts. Where the poster is Earl Geddes himself, those are primary (his own words); where they are other community members or BEM-sim screenshots, they are simulation evidence + community convention, flagged as such. The JAES 1993 aperture-diffraction formula and its "affects FR and directivity" wording are a community transcription of a paywalled paper (correct page range 214–230, not 214–231) and should be confirmed against the PDF before publication-grade citation. Toole & Olive (1988) runs to p. 142 (not 141).
- **Your specific driver/waveguide:** a well-terminated R-OSSE mouth (the whole point of the roll-back profile) should produce *little* mouth-edge diffraction, which paradoxically raises the probability that the four features are either (a) DE250 phase-plug/cavity modes (angle-independent, EQ-able) or (b) gating residuals — both of which your persistence test will separate cleanly. If the ±7/±22° overlays show the features are stable and minimum-phase, keep gentle LW-fit filters; if they wash out or shift, remove them.
