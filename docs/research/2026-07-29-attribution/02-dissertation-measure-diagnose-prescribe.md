## TL;DR

* The prior art already contains everything JTS needs to build a mechanism-attribution stage, but no shipped consumer system does the full job: Trinnov and Genelec GRADE are the closest models — they name findings (per-driver delay, level, polarity, early-reflection structure, time-of-flight, RT60) and, crucially, GRADE routes findings to non-DSP recommendations (move the speaker, add absorption) rather than blindly EQ-ing. Copy that "diagnose-then-route-to-fix-class" spine; skip the fully-automated black boxes (Sonos/HomePod, Audyssey) that hide their reasoning and overcorrect.
* Three discriminating probes give the most attribution leverage for a phone-mic + conductor architecture you already have: (1) the reverse-null polarity-flip test at the crossover (separates phase-tracking/delay/polarity from slope error; aim for a deep, centered null, −20 to −30 dB when aligned); (2) the position-variance test across your existing 8-position cloud (a comb that rides with the speaker is source-fixed horn/diffraction; one that moves is a room reflection); and (3) the Farina harmonic-IR separation you get for free from the sweep you already run (attributes nonlinearity to a driver and onset level).
* The motivating 2 kHz case is almost certainly two different mechanisms stacked: the −4 dB notch at Fc is a crossover-region phase/lobing/slope problem (fixable by delay/polarity/EQ or reclassifiable as vertical-lobing power loss), while the 8.4/11.4/14.8 kHz comb (~3 kHz spacing ⇒ ~0.3 ms ⇒ ~10 cm path difference) is a source-fixed CD-horn throat/mouth reflection that is document-as-physics, not EQ — EQ into those nulls only burns headroom. Note: 0.3 ms corresponds to a ~10 cm round-trip path, i.e. roughly a 5 cm-deep horn, not a 10 cm-deep horn.

## Key Findings

1. Shipped diagnosis-first architectures. Only two systems truly "diagnose before prescribing" in a way JTS should emulate: Trinnov's Optimizer (which decomposes the acoustic path into active-crossover, loudspeaker response, early reflections, room modes, and power response, and separately time/level-aligns each source) and Genelec's GLM/GRADE (which produces a colour-coded, standards-referenced report that explicitly separates DSP-correctable from placement/treatment problems). Dirac, Acourate/Audiolense, and REW encode "EQ cannot fix this" knowledge in workflow rather than in an automated diagnosis. Sonos/HomePod are the zero-skill, zero-transparency extreme.
2. The discriminating-probe catalog. The single highest-leverage tool in speaker-building practice is the reverse-null test; the highest-leverage tool JTS uniquely can exploit is position-variance across its cloud; and the cheapest is Farina harmonic separation (already latent in the ESS sweep).
3. Signature → mechanism → fix-class mapping. A crossover suckout has at least four distinct causes (inter-driver delay, polarity error, vertical lobing off the design axis, acoustic-slope/overlap error) that phase + multi-position data can disambiguate. Comb filtering splits into source-fixed (horn/diffraction/edge) vs room reflections. SBIR/floor-bounce dips are placement, not speaker, and are actively harmful to EQ.
4. Frames. CTA-2034 spinorama science (Toole/Olive) shows the listening window predicts preference and that no single curve dominates; for a desk/tabletop near-field speaker the direct sound dominates, so the linearization layer should target flat direct/listening-window response and the preference layer a gentle downward in-room tilt.
5. Automated diagnosis prior art. Klippel is the paradigm (fit a physical model, identify which parameter is out of spec), but its lab methods only partially transfer to phone-mic in-room use. The cautionary catalog (Audyssey HF overcorrection, Dirac phase-break failures) argues for confidence-gated, conservative correction.
6. Communicating to non-experts. GRADE's colour-coded summary + drill-down + "consult a professional" escape hatch is the template; the honest-refusal policy ("prescribe only above confidence X, else recommend the disambiguating test") is directly implementable given JTS's probe toolbox.

## Details

### Q1 — Diagnosis-first architectures in shipped systems

Trinnov Optimizer. Trinnov's public technology pages and Optimizer reference manual describe an architecture that is exactly the "decompose then attribute" spine JTS wants. The Optimizer "distinguishes and separately processes every single element of the acoustic path, specifically: Active Crossovers, loudspeaker response, early reflections, room modes and power Response of the room." It measures each loudspeaker/subwoofer individually to perform automatic level alignment and "applies delays to time-align the system," using "state-of-the-art time-frequency algorithms" to "compensate for direct sound, first reflections, late reflections/reverberation and room modes." The manual's most important sentence for JTS is a refusal principle: "All the subtlety of the Optimizer resides in knowing which defects may be corrected without creating additional problems." Trinnov uses a proprietary 4-capsule tetrahedral 3D microphone to build a 3D acoustic map (enabling true spatial/directional analysis), corrects amplitude and phase (including group delay), and surfaces "hundreds, if not thousands, of acoustic data points" as before/after acoustic graphs so the user (or a calibrator using a separate measurement system) can verify.

* Named findings: per-driver delay, per-driver level, absolute level, phase/group-delay, and the decomposition into direct/early/late/modal/power. Trinnov markets automatic time-alignment "with more precision than a human could achieve with a tape measure."
* Detection method: time-frequency (time-domain) analysis rather than steady-state pink-noise RTA; cross-correlation/excess-phase specifics are not disclosed in the public manual (confidence: medium — the manual confirms the categories and the time-domain philosophy but not the exact estimator).

Copy / Skip / Why. COPY the explicit path decomposition (crossover vs loudspeaker vs early-reflection vs mode vs power) as JTS's top-level mechanism ontology, and copy the refusal principle verbatim as a design tenet. COPY the "show before/after phase and time graphs" transparency. SKIP the 3D-mic dependency (JTS is a single phone mic; a tetrahedral capture is out of scope) — but note this is precisely the capability JTS approximates with its multi-position cloud. WHY: Trinnov proves that naming the owning subsystem before filtering is a shipped, respected approach, and that the hard part is the "which defects are correctable" gate — the exact gap JTS is filling.

Genelec GLM / AutoCal + GRADE. GRADE is the closest consumer analogue to "diagnose before prescribe." It is a reporting layer on top of AutoCal that generates a 30-page (stereo) to 100+-page (immersive) PDF. Its structure is directly copyable:

* A colour-coded summary table: green = "excellent system performance," yellow = "good... but some improvements may be possible," red = "improvements could significantly enhance your system."
* Frequency-domain: measured/filter/corrected response overlays; low-frequency −6 dB extension point vs anechoic reference; a peaks-and-notches graph explicitly labelled as "room influences," with guidance like a "significant dip at 75 Hz, perhaps caused by a reflection from the back wall at a distance of approx. 1.14 m (quarter-wavelength-rule). A position closer to the wall could improve the response." This is a worked mechanism attribution with a distance estimate and a placement prescription — exactly the JTS pattern.
* Time-domain: time-of-flight per channel (compensated by delay + gain); an "all early reflections" list, each compared to direct level; and "early vs late sound" (energy within 20 ms vs after).
* Time-frequency: RT60 per octave, waterfall (ringing/resonance), wavelet (higher-resolution reflection detail).
* ITU-R BS.1116 compliance checks: operational room response within tolerance bands; early reflections vs the −10 dB rule; RT60 vs volume-dependent target.

Crucially, GRADE routes findings to non-DSP actions: it recommends absorbers for early reflections and long RT60, bass traps for low-frequency decay, repositioning for boundary notches, and vertical (not horizontal) orientation for two-way monitors to avoid the crossover-region level drop when the listener moves sideways. Genelec describes GRADE's purpose as "to effectively fine tune acoustic treatment, adjust monitor and listener positioning, and optimise Bass Management." Independent review (Sound On Sound, GLM 4.2) confirms the report "goes into much greater depth than I expected... any potential problem areas are clearly highlighted," and that "the GRADE Report would be an excellent document to share with [an acoustician] as a starting point" — i.e., an explicit hand-off to a human expert. GLM auto-compensates the desktop/console boost around 160–200 Hz.

COPY the colour-coded three-tier summary, the per-finding drill-down, the explicit routing of each finding to DSP vs placement vs treatment, and the worked "dip at X Hz ⇒ boundary at Y m ⇒ move closer" attribution style. COPY the ITU-style tolerance bands as the "is this even a problem" gate. SKIP the paid-report gating and the RT60/waterfall/wavelet machinery that needs a long ungated tail (JTS is gated quasi-anechoic at ~7 ms and near-field; late-decay analysis is low-value for a tabletop). WHY: GRADE is the canonical, user-facing "honest diagnosis" report; its language and routing are the template for Q6.

Dirac Live. Dirac uses mixed-phase correction (a non-causal look-ahead FIR + causal IIR) to reduce time-domain distortion, not just magnitude — its whitepaper "On Room Correction and Equalization of Sound Systems" is candid about limits: information-bearing reflections that vary with position cannot and should not be corrected ("the information-bearing reflections are not possible to improve, simply because the reason that they bear information is their variation with position"), and it warns that group delay "only measures the delay of the envelope... It is nonsense to interpret this as a physical delay." Dirac explicitly declines to fix fundamental placement/room problems ("Dirac Live cannot overcome fundamental acoustic problems stemming from poor room design or speaker placement"), and its helpdesk documents a hard refusal: "If a certain speaker, usually a subwoofer... has a great delay (>20ms), some Dirac-enabled devices cannot adjust the other speakers to match this delay," with the prescribed fix being physical — "Move the speakers closer together to minimize delay due to physical distance. Remeasure your listening space with Dirac Live when you have minimized delays." Dirac also flags measurements as "imprecise measurement is usable but cannot contribute to phase correction" — a shipped example of confidence-tiered use of a measurement.

Copy / Skip / Why. COPY the position-variance principle (what varies with position is room and largely uncorrectable; what is stable is the speaker) — this is the theoretical backbone of JTS's source-vs-room discriminator. COPY the "measurement usable for magnitude but not phase" confidence tiering. SKIP mixed-phase FIR implementation detail (JTS owns its own filter stack). WHY: Dirac gives you the rigorous justification, from a correction vendor, for not correcting position-variant features.

Acourate / Audiolense. These are expert manual workflows where time alignment is an explicit, separate step the operator performs and diagnoses between stages. In the canonical Acourate walkthrough the high-level steps are: create the digital XO, measure, linearize each driver in its passband, measure the inter-driver delay from the impulse peaks, then design an all-pass to fit the measured excess-phase, then correct the room's frequency and excess-phase response — checking IACC and listening between steps. The operator manually diagnoses driver phase (rotating the pulse to 0 to read residual phase rotation, e.g. "180° at 230 Hz") and decides whether individual driver linearization is even needed ("If the drivers measure ±2 dB near field in their passband, then one can skip the individual driver linearization step").

SKIP the manual all-pass fitting (JTS auto-fits). WHY: validates JTS's layered stack and shows experts diagnose between steps, not just at the end.

REW. REW encodes the "EQ cannot fix this" knowledge procedurally. Its driver-time-alignment guidance (via miniDSP) uses the impulse-peak time difference between drivers and then the reverse-null check: "To verify that the two drivers are in phase, invert one driver and run a measurement sweep. If the drivers are in phase and have the same amplitude, they will cancel at the crossover frequency and create a null." Its Alignment Tool computes sub/main delay and an "Align phase at cursor" at the crossover from measured phase. Practitioner guidance is explicit that time alignment (broadband delay) and phase alignment (frequency-specific crossover-region cancellation) are different problems — "Mixing them up leads to the wrong fix applied at the wrong step."

Copy / Skip / Why. COPY the reverse-null as a first-class probe and the delay-vs-phase distinction as two separate named mechanisms with two separate fixes. WHY: REW is the free tool your DIY audience already knows; matching its vocabulary lowers the trust barrier.

### Q2 — The discriminating-probe catalog

Reverse-null (polarity-flip) crossover test — highest leverage. Flip one driver's polarity and re-measure; at the crossover the depth of the resulting null measures phase-tracking quality. Practitioner consensus: aim for a deep, centered null of roughly −20 to −30 dB when the drivers are well phase-aligned (one experienced builder "aiming usually for −25/−30 dB"; another recommends getting the phase curves "basically on top of each other in the overlap area, maybe down to −15 dB or more"). Key discriminators embedded in the practice: a shallow or shifted null "usually means acoustic centers, levels, or slopes are not tracking" — i.e., it separates a clean delay/polarity problem (deep null achievable by delay/polarity) from a slope/level mismatch (null cannot be made deep). Caveat from the literature: the deep-null check is only valid for in-phase (even-order LR) crossovers; odd-order Butterworth won't null. This exactly fits JTS's LR4 at 2 kHz. Capture cost: one extra solo-ish sweep with inverted polarity. Phone-mic feasible: yes (relative measurement, level-robust).

Solo-driver captures vs summed. Per-way response plus anchored relative arrival time is the foundation for all crossover diagnosis; JTS already has µs-anchored solo captures. This is the reference against which the reverse-null and lobing tests are interpreted.

Multi-level (level-invariance) test. Linear mechanisms — interference nulls, response errors, diffraction combs — are invariant in dB with drive level; compression, port chuffing/noise, and suspension nonlinearity are not. A two-level protocol (e.g., a lower and a higher SNR-solved drive, the levels JTS already solves) with a per-bin dB-delta threshold cleanly separates "linear feature (attribute to interference/response)" from "nonlinear feature (attribute to a driver/port at an onset level)." Deltas within measurement repeatability = linear; deltas that grow with level = nonlinear. Phone-mic feasible with caveats (phone mic AGC/compression must be defeated; the phone's own nonlinearity can masquerade — flag this).

ESS harmonic separation (Farina) — free from the sweep you already run. Farina's exponential-sine-sweep method — Angelo Farina, "Simultaneous Measurement of Impulse Response and Distortion with a Swept-Sine Technique," AES 108th Convention, Paris, Feb 2000, Paper No. 5093 (Univ. of Parma), refined 2007 — places the harmonic-distortion impulse responses at negative delays (earlier than the linear IR), so orders 2, 3, 5… separate out and can be windowed individually from the same sweep. The method is explicitly "applicable to loudspeakers... but also to room acoustics measurements." Diagnosis this yields: which harmonic orders are present, and (with the multi-level test) the onset level; combined with solo captures, which driver is distorting. Limits: recent work (Torras 2011, Kemp 2011) shows some odd-order distortion still contaminates the causal part; and phone mics add their own harmonic distortion that sets a noise floor on what JTS can attribute to the speaker. Confidence: high on method, medium on phone-mic sensitivity.

Repeat-capture variance. Re-measuring and differencing detects time-variance (HVAC, traffic, wind, someone moving) vs stable features — a prerequisite gate before any attribution. JTS's cloud already supports this. COPY as a validity gate.

Nearfield vs farfield (Keele). Keele's 1974 nearfield technique (D.B. Keele, Jr., "Low-Frequency Loudspeaker Assessment by Nearfield Sound-Pressure Measurement," JAES Vol. 22 No. 3, April 1974; mic within 0.11× the effective radius, ~0.275" for a 6.5" driver) gives the driver's low-frequency behavior independent of room, splice-able to the gated farfield in the overlap region (roughly 1/T to c/πM). Validity limits: nearfield assumes 2π/half-space loading and omits baffle diffraction/baffle step, so the splice must correct for it; and it is only valid where the diaphragm is a rigid piston. For JTS's ~5.5" woofer on a tabletop this extends LF diagnosis below the ~7 ms gate limit but must be flagged as half-space and baffle-step-uncorrected.

Gated vs ungated / windowed comparison — the source-vs-room discriminator. Comparing gated (direct) to ungated (direct+reflected) energy, and especially checking whether a null's frequency moves with mic/speaker position, is the canonical discriminator between "comb rides with the speaker" (source-fixed: horn, cabinet-edge diffraction) and "comb is the room" (position-dependent). This is JTS's single most valuable native capability because it already has an 8-position cloud: a feature stable in frequency across all 8 positions is source-fixed; a feature that walks in frequency as position changes is a room reflection/SBIR.

Ground-plane measurement. Worth the hassle only when you need a reflection-free half-space farfield low-frequency measurement outdoors or in a large space; it inherently includes the floor and avoids the nearfield diffraction correction. For a tabletop smart speaker measured in-situ this is generally not worth it — SKIP, document as out-of-scope.

### Q3 — Signature → mechanism → fix-class mapping

Crossover-region suckout taxonomy. On an LR4 design a −4 dB notch at Fc can be:

1. Inter-driver time offset (delay) — drivers' acoustic centers not co-planar. Signature: on-axis null with asymmetric phase; the reverse-null can be made deep by adding delay. Fix-class: delay.
2. Polarity error — one driver inverted. Signature: on the normal (non-inverted) measurement you get a deep null at Fc, and flipping polarity fills it. Fix-class: polarity.
3. Vertical lobing (off design axis) — with driver spacing ~one wavelength at Fc (for a 2 kHz XO, ~17 cm; a 5.5" woofer + horn stack can approach this), the combined lobe tilts off the design axis and you get a suckout when the mic is above/below it. The Rane/Lipshitz "lobing error" analysis: LR (in-phase) crossovers keep the lobe on-axis with no peaking only if the drivers are time-aligned; failure to time-align "defeats this zero lobing error aspect." Odd-order filters tilt the lobe (downward for BW3, upward if tweeter inverted). Signature: the notch depth/frequency changes with vertical mic angle and is accompanied by a power-response dip even when on-axis is flat. Fix-class: physical (driver spacing/aim) or measure-differently (measure on the true design axis); EQ is harmful.
4. Acoustic-slope / unfitted-overlap error — the two acoustic slopes don't sum flat because the native driver rolloffs plus filters don't realize LR4 acoustically. Signature: reverse-null cannot be made deep at any delay; magnitude error is broad, not a sharp interference notch. Fix-class: EQ / re-fit the crossover.

The disambiguation recipe (from REW/VituixCAD practice): use magnitude + phase from solo captures + the reverse-null (depth achievable?) + multi-position/vertical data (does it move with angle?). JTS has all four inputs.

Important LR4 subtlety for the motivating case: an LR4 crossover has an inherent −6 dB-crossover power-response behavior — practitioners note "LR4 has a power hole at crossover just by its −6 dB crossover nature, though it measures flat on axis," especially where a beaming woofer hands to a controlled-directivity horn. So a multi-position power-mean dip at 2 kHz that the on-axis/listening-window does not show is very likely a directivity/power-response feature, not an error to EQ — this is directly your #1859 frame question and the desk-point-vs-8-position 7.7 dB HF disagreement. Fix-class: document-as-physics (and choose the listening-window frame for the linearization target).

Comb filtering: source-fixed vs room. The primary-source dive is decisive here. The CD-horn interference mechanism traces to David W. Gunness (Electro-Voice, later Eastern Acoustic Works), whose early patent US 4,685,532A describes the diffraction-slot geometry causing "drop-out of the high frequency response along the central axis of the horn due to interference between the wavefronts," and whose corrective time-domain technique was commercialized as "Gunness Focusing" at EAW. The mechanism is stated verbatim in US 8,081,766 B2 (the patent behind his AES 2005 transient-response paper): "Constant directivity horns often employ a diffraction slot... The exit of this slot represents a severe discontinuity. A discontinuity in a horn's expansion produces a reflection. A fraction of the sound power reverses course and returns to the compression driver where it is partially absorbed and partially re-emitted, often several milliseconds late. This process is regenerative, once again producing a decaying series of arrivals." Critically for attribution, the patent establishes these reflections are a two-port, direction-independent property of the device — "the transient response distortion... does not vary with respect to a direction away from the loudspeaker" — which is why it is source-fixed and correctable in common but directionality itself "cannot be corrected everywhere." Earl Geddes's Higher-Order-Mode (HOM) theory (Audio Transducers, GedLee, 2002, Ch. 6; "Acoustic Waveguide Theory," JAES 1989 and "…Revisited," JAES 1993) adds that abrupt slope/area discontinuities and diffraction slots spawn HOMs and standing waves ("the diffraction slot causes a large amount of the wavefront to be reflected back down the horn creating a standing wave and an acoustic resonance, which is highly audible as sound coloration... HOM are also created at any slope or area discontinuities"), and that these non-retracing reflections are non-minimum-phase — a reason EQ cannot cleanly invert them. Corroborating patents (US 8,477,979; US 11,310,587) state the throat/slot area discontinuity "will produce a reflected wave... [causing] both time domain distortion as well as a change in the amplitude versus frequency response" and "ripples... in the frequency response resulting in comb filtering."

Motivating-case arithmetic — with a correction. The comb-null spacing relation is Δf = c / (path-length difference) (Everest, Master Handbook of Acoustics). The observed nulls at 8.4 / 11.4 / 14.8 kHz are spaced ~3.0–3.4 kHz, consistent with a delay of ~0.3 ms (1/0.3 ms ≈ 3.3 kHz) and a path-length difference of ~0.3 ms × 343 m/s ≈ 10.3 cm. This 10 cm is a path-length difference, not the horn depth: a round-trip reflection inside a horn traverses ~2× the depth, so 0.3 ms/10 cm implies a horn roughly 5 cm deep (a 10 cm deep horn would give ~0.58 ms and ~1.7 kHz null spacing). Either way, the feature is horn-geometry-shaped and matches the CD-horn throat/mouth reflection mechanism. Fix-class: document-as-physics (and if audible, a mild, carefully-bounded transient/preconditioning filter of the Gunness-Focusing type — but never narrow EQ boost into the nulls, which burns headroom into an interference cancellation that will re-appear as the reflected energy is boosted too).

SBIR / floor and desk bounce. For a tabletop speaker the desk is the dominant near boundary. SBIR dips follow f = c/(2·pathdiff) (the widely-cited f = 172/x m form for the first null with x the direct-vs-reflected path difference in metres); the desk bounce for a speaker sitting ~20–40 cm above a desk lands the first cancellation in the low-mids/midbass. These are placement, not speaker, and the literature is unanimous that EQ cannot fix SBIR ("boosting the cancellation frequency will also amplify the reflection causing the interference"). Discriminator: SBIR nulls move with speaker/boundary distance (position-variant in JTS's cloud) and are computable from geometry. Fix-class: physical (move the speaker/raise it/tilt it) or document-as-physics; EQ actively harmful.

Baffle step. A ~6 dB broad shelf (not a notch) as radiation transitions from 2π (baffle-supported) to 4π, with the transition frequency set by baffle width (≈ c/π·W). Signature: broad, smooth, minimum-phase level shift — distinguishable from an interference notch by being broad, monotonic, and not position-variant. Fix-class: EQ (this is one of the few things EQ should fix), or it's already baked into the linearization target.

Port / passive-radiator resonances and chuffing. Port resonances appear as sharp response artifacts (and a nearfield port measurement shows the tuning-frequency dip in the woofer nearfield); chuffing/port noise is a nonlinear, level-dependent turbulence signature that the level-invariance test flags. Fix-class: physical (port geometry) or measure-differently; not EQ.

### Q4 — Frames and what predicts preference

CTA-2034 / spinorama (Toole, Olive). The Olive preference model — Sean Olive, "A Multiple Regression Model for Predicting Loudspeaker Preference Using Objective Measurements," AES Conventions 116 & 117 (2004), developed and tested across 19 listening tests on 70 loudspeakers — predicts double-blind preference rankings with ~0.86 correlation from the anechoic spin data. In Olive's own words the model "will predict the results of the listening tests with 86% accuracy based on 70 different loudspeakers tested"; ANSI/CTA-2034A frames the spinorama as containing "the information from which one can predict double-blind listener loudspeaker preference rankings with a statistical accuracy of 0.86 for loudspeakers with varied bass capability." The key perceptual finding is that no single curve dominates at all frequencies — perception weights direct sound, early reflections, and sound power according to room reflectivity. For interpretation: flat/smooth on-axis and listening-window response is essential; smooth, gradually-sloping early-reflection and sound-power curves are beneficial; large peaks/dips signal resonances. For a desk/tabletop near-field speaker the listener is close (near field), so the direct sound / listening window dominates and the reverberant/sound-power contribution is relatively smaller than for a living-room stereo pair — meaning JTS's linearization layer should target a flat listening-window (direct) response, and the desk bounce (an early reflection) should be handled by placement/physical measures, not by EQ-flattening the one-point desk measurement.

Power-response dip at crossover (the #1859 frame question). As established in Q3, an LR4 handoff between a beaming ~5.5" woofer (beaming by ~2 kHz, since ka≈1 around there) and a controlled-directivity horn produces a genuine power/DI feature at Fc that the listening window need not show. When JTS's 8-position cloud (power-mean-like) disagrees with a single desk on-axis point by up to 7.7 dB in the HF, the correct interpretation is that these are two different frames measuring two different physical quantities, not a measurement error — the on-axis/window frame is what predicts preference and should drive the linearization target; the power dip should be named as directivity physics and either left alone or addressed by overlap/driver-spacing choices, never EQ-boosted (which would wreck the on-axis response to chase a power number).

Low-point-count directivity estimation. An 8-position phone cloud is sparse, but it can estimate a DI proxy at the crossover sufficient to discriminate lobing/power-dip from a true on-axis error: if the on-axis (or best-axis) response is flat at Fc while the cloud mean shows a dip, that gap is an elevated-DI/power-hole signature; if both show the same notch, it's a genuine summation/phase error. This is a coarse but decisive binary. Prior art on formal sparse spatial sampling for full directivity is thin at N=8 (confidence: low that JTS can estimate a full polar map; high that it can compute the window-vs-power delta that answers the lobing-vs-error question).

Target curves per layer. The five-decades-consistent finding (B&K 1974 → Toole/Olive → Dolby) is that the preferred in-room response slopes gently downward (~1 dB/octave, roughly −6 to −10 dB across the band), and Olive is explicit that "a flat in-room target response is clearly not the optimal target curve for room equalization." But the optimal tilt depends on directivity and room reflectivity: Olive notes that in a dead room or with a high-directivity speaker, "the in-room response will represent a higher proportion of the direct sound, which should be flat" — so a strong downward tilt is wrong there. For JTS's layered stack: the linearization layer should target flat anechoic/direct (listening-window) response; the preference layer on top should apply the downward tilt, with the tilt amount reduced for JTS's near-field/high-directivity-horn case (because the near-field direct sound already dominates). This cleanly separates "correct the speaker to neutral" from "season to taste."

### Q5 — Automated diagnosis prior art

Klippel as paradigm. Klippel's approach is stimulus → fit a physical model → identify which parameter is out of spec. For irregular defects (rub & buzz), the TRF-Pro method computes the crest factor and peak of the higher-order harmonic distortion; because defect distortion is impulsive it has a high crest factor (a fixed threshold ≈ 10 dB reliably separates it from regular nonlinear distortion), and plotting crest factor vs voice-coil displacement localizes when in the stroke the defect fires, "giving further indications of the physical cause." "Meta-hearing" actively compensates regular distortion so a loose-particle spike shows against a PASS/FAIL limit. Large-signal identification (LSI) fits Bl(x), suspension, thermal parameters.

* What transfers to phone-mic in-room JTS: the crest-factor / impulsive-distortion detector transfers well (it needs only the harmonic IRs JTS already gets from Farina, and it's relatively robust to a modest mic); the concept of "fit a model, report the out-of-spec parameter with the physical cause" is the exact philosophy JTS should adopt.
* What does not transfer: displacement-domain analysis, LSI, and thermal-compression parameter fits need lab control (known drive, laser/current sensing) JTS doesn't have. Attribute nonlinearity to a driver and onset level, not to a fitted Bl(x) curve. (Confidence: high — the Klippel app notes are explicit about what the measurement requires.)

Auto-alignment / auto-polarity detection. The dominant published methods are cross-correlation of impulse responses (peak of the cumulative cross-correlation gives inter-channel/inter-driver delay — see the general delay-detection patent literature) and impulse-peak/excess-phase methods (rotate the IR to zero and read residual phase; minimum-phase extraction from magnitude to isolate excess phase = the true acoustic delay). REW/Acourate operationalize these. Robustness/failure modes: cross-correlation fails when the two signals have very different spectral content (woofer vs tweeter share little band at the crossover, so correlation peaks are broad/ambiguous) — hence the practice of using a common broadband reference and reading the impulse peak difference instead; group-delay misreads (Dirac's warning) can point the delay the wrong way. JTS's µs-anchored captures sidestep the worst of this. (Confidence: medium-high on methods; the exact Trinnov estimator is undisclosed.)

Automated defect / room-vs-speaker attribution (academic). Klippel's production-line and QC papers are the mature model-based defect-detection literature. Formal automated room-vs-speaker attribution as a published algorithm is thin — the closest shipped logic is Dirac's position-variance principle (variant = room) and Trinnov's path decomposition, both design heuristics rather than published classifiers. This is genuinely a gap in the prior art, and therefore an area where JTS's position-variance-across-cloud test is a modest novel contribution rather than a re-implementation. (Confidence: high that this is under-published; flag as JTS's differentiator.)

Documented failure modes of automated correction (the cautionary catalog).

* Audyssey: well-documented high-frequency overcorrection producing a "dead/dull/muffled" result; forcing target curves and boosts users can't easily override; the midrange-compensation dip applied when unwanted. Lesson: correction must get broader with frequency (individual modes stop dominating above the transition/Schroeder region), and HF should be trimmed conservatively.
* Dirac: phase "break" failures when a speaker's delay exceeds handling (>20 ms) — refuses and tells you to move the speaker; the Arcam Bass-Control export bug that shipped flat instead of optimized phase coefficients (a reminder to verify applied filters, which JTS already does).
* General: all these systems will happily EQ a position-variant interference null (SBIR, lobing) and burn headroom — the exact failure JTS's attribution stage exists to prevent.

Lesson for JTS: confidence-gate and frequency-taper the correction; never boost into a null you haven't proven is minimum-phase and source-fixed-but-correctable.

### Q6 — Communicating diagnosis to non-experts

GRADE is the model: a colour-coded three-tier summary (green/yellow/red) for instant triage, then optional drill-down with plain-language, physically-grounded explanations ("dip at 75 Hz, likely a back-wall reflection at ~1.14 m; move closer"), and an explicit escape hatch to a human expert (the report is designed to be shared with an acoustician "as a starting point"). Genelec pairs it with a "slow listening" disclaimer — an honest admission that measurement isn't the whole story. Dirac demonstrates confidence tiering in the wild ("measurement usable but cannot contribute to phase correction").

For JTS the implementable policy is: prescribe an automated fix only when the mechanism is attributed above a confidence threshold; below it, recommend the specific disambiguating probe (e.g., "the 2 kHz dip could be inter-driver delay or vertical lobing — run the polarity-flip test and a vertical-offset capture to decide"). This "refuse-and-recommend-the-test" policy is well-supported by adjacent honest-refusal UX and by Trinnov's stated principle that the whole art is "knowing which defects may be corrected without creating additional problems." Confidence should be surfaced as plain words (e.g., "confident / likely / unsure — needs one more measurement"), never as a bare number.

## Recommendations

The attribution stage the prior art would build for JTS — staged, with the thresholds that would change the plan.

Stage 0 — Validity gate (build first; cheap, already have inputs). Run repeat-capture variance across the cloud. If a feature is time-variant (differs beyond measurement repeatability between repeats), label it "environment, not speaker/room" and exclude from attribution. Threshold to proceed: feature stable across ≥2 repeats within ~1 dB.

Stage 1 — Prioritize the top 3–5 mechanisms (highest value first for a phone-mic + conductor with µs anchoring, solo captures, clouds, SNR-solved levels, and excluded-band/τ machinery):

1. Source-fixed comb vs room reflection — via position-variance across the 8-position cloud (stable frequency ⇒ source/horn/diffraction ⇒ document-as-physics; walks with position ⇒ room/SBIR ⇒ placement). This directly resolves your 8.4/11.4/14.8 kHz comb.
2. Crossover-region suckout: delay vs polarity vs lobing vs slope — via the reverse-null polarity-flip probe + solo-capture phase + a vertical-offset capture. Resolves the −4 dB 2 kHz notch into a fix-class.
3. Power-dip vs on-axis error at Fc — via the listening-window-vs-cloud-mean delta (DI proxy). Resolves #1859: if window flat but cloud dips, name it directivity physics; don't EQ.
4. Nonlinearity attribution — via Farina harmonic-IR separation + the two-level invariance test (which driver, what onset level; separates compression/chuffing from linear features).
5. SBIR/desk-bounce (placement) — via geometry prediction + position-variance; route to physical/document, never EQ.

Stage 2 — Route each attributed finding to a fix-class exactly as GRADE does: delay / polarity / EQ / physical / document-as-physics / measure-differently. Encode the hard rule from Dirac/SBIR literature: position-variant nulls and source-fixed interference combs are never EQ-boosted.

Stage 3 — Confidence-gate the prescription (Q6 policy). Above threshold, auto-apply and verify (JTS already verifies). Below threshold, refuse and recommend the single disambiguating probe with plain-language confidence. Thresholds that change the plan: if the reverse-null can be driven below ~−20 dB by delay alone ⇒ high-confidence "delay" fix; if it can't be driven below ~−10 dB at any delay ⇒ "slope/level error, re-fit crossover"; if the notch frequency moves >~10% with vertical angle ⇒ "vertical lobing, measure/aim on design axis, do not EQ."

Stage 4 — Target per layer. Linearization layer → flat listening-window (direct) response, using nearfield+gated splice for the woofer LF (baffle-step-corrected). Preference layer → gentle downward tilt, reduced from the ~1 dB/octave living-room norm because JTS is near-field/high-directivity (direct sound dominates).

The two or three probes to build first: (1) reverse-null polarity-flip, (2) position-variance across the existing cloud, (3) Farina harmonic separation from the existing sweep. All three reuse machinery JTS already has; together they cover mechanisms 1–4 above.

## Caveats

* Shipped vs academic separation. Trinnov, GRADE, Dirac, Sonos, Audyssey claims are from vendor manuals/whitepapers/help-desks and reputable reviews (shipped evidence). Klippel methods are vendor app-notes/AES papers (shipped instrument, but lab-grade). Toole/Olive preference correlation (0.86, from Olive's 2004 AES multiple-regression model) and CTA-2034 are peer-reviewed/standardized. Geddes HOM and Gunness horn-reflection mechanisms are AES-paper/patent primary sources. The position-variance room-vs-speaker classifier as a published automated algorithm is thin — treated here as a design heuristic and a JTS differentiator, not established prior art.
* Motivating-case arithmetic correction. 0.3 ms ⇒ ~10 cm is a path-length difference, implying a horn ~5 cm deep (round-trip), not 10 cm deep; a genuinely 10 cm-deep horn predicts ~0.58 ms and ~1.7 kHz null spacing. The observed ~3 kHz null spacing is consistent with ~0.3 ms/~10 cm path. Verify the physical horn depth before finalizing the "horn-shaped" claim.
* Gunness patent number. The verbatim "several milliseconds late... decaying series of arrivals" quote is confirmed from US 8,081,766 B2; the earlier Electro-Voice CD-horn interference patent is US 4,685,532A. Confirm both numbers against USPTO before publishing the design doc.
* Phone-mic limits. Farina harmonic separation and the level-invariance test are bounded by the phone mic's own distortion and any AGC/compression; JTS must defeat AGC and treat the phone's harmonic floor as the sensitivity limit. Nearfield splices assume half-space and omit baffle diffraction (must be corrected).
* Undisclosed internals. Trinnov's exact delay/polarity estimator and Sonos's/HomePod's inference are not publicly documented; those descriptions are at the "named capability" level, not algorithmic.
* Reverse-null validity. The deep-null check is only valid for in-phase (even-order LR) crossovers — fine for JTS's LR4, but the stage must not apply the same null-depth thresholds to any odd-order or mixed topologies.
* Contested/forum-tier points (reverse-null target depth of −20 to −30 dB; LR4 "power hole" framing) reflect strong practitioner consensus rather than a single authoritative citation; treated as engineering heuristics.
