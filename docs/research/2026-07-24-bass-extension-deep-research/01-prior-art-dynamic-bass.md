## TL;DR
- **Volume-indexed, model-based LF extension is well-established prior art and is exactly what ships in modern smart speakers.** The strongest direct precedent is Google's own "Dynamically extending loudspeaker capabilities" (US 10,200,003 / US 10,666,217) and Microsoft's US 12,342,139, which literally schedule Linkwitz Transforms by volume/headroom. The user's feedforward, no-sensing, per-unit-commissioned approach is technically sound and matches how B&O ABL, Devialet SAM, TI PurePath, and NXP/Goodix "sensorless" model modes behave — but the specific combination is heavily patented, so the design freedom is in *implementation and per-unit commissioning*, not the concept.
- **Two mechanistic families dominate:** (a) *signal/envelope-driven* protection that pulls back bass as the driver approaches modeled excursion/thermal limits (B&O ABL, Devialet SAM, TI/NXP smart amps, Dynaudio sliding high-pass), and (b) *volume-knob-indexed* extension/loudness (Sonos loudness; Google/Microsoft LT scheduling). The user's system deliberately fuses both: volume-indexed target selection with per-unit measured excursion ceilings.
- **Transition-dynamics guidance converges on asymmetry:** fast attack (single-digit to low-tens of milliseconds) to catch excursion risk, slow release (hundreds of ms to a few seconds) to avoid audible pumping/breathing; move the LF corner/shelf in small steps with hysteresis, and crossfade biquad coefficient changes rather than switching them.

## Key Findings
1. **The exact concept — volume-dependent Linkwitz Transform scheduling — is patented and shipping.** Microsoft US 12,342,139 B2 claims a volume-indexed lookup table pairing a Linkwitz Transform with a multiband compressor per volume level; Google US 10,200,003/10,666,217 claim modifying LT gain and corner frequency in response to available headroom (driven by volume and amplifier state). This is not marketing — it is granted claim language, and the Microsoft patent runs to **December 12, 2041**.
2. **Most "intelligent bass" marketing is signal-dependent protection, not volume-knob loudness.** B&O is explicit that ABL is *signal-dependent*, not volume-dependent, and is about preventing distortion, not equal-loudness compensation. Sonos "Loudness," by contrast, *is* volume-knob-indexed but is equal-loudness compensation, not protection.
3. **Real numbers exist mainly in the semiconductor datasheets and pro-audio limiter app notes**, not in hi-fi marketing. TI PurePath and NXP/Goodix TFA parts contain the most concrete model structure; Linea Research and Analog Devices/Maxim app notes give the most concrete time-constant and sliding-high-pass guidance.
4. **The "sensorless" excursion limiter the user is building is a documented, credible technique** (diyAudio's sensorless DSP cone excursion limiter using Small's transfer-function excursion prediction) and mirrors the *feedforward* mode of smart amps — distinct from the current/voltage *sensing* modes the user cannot use.

### The NXP/Goodix sensing-vs-feedforward distinction (load-bearing for relevance)
- **Sensing mode (NOT usable by the user):** TFA9887/TFA9890 use a current-sensing (9890 adds voltage) class-D amp to measure *actual* membrane excursion and voice-coil temperature and adapt the loop for aging, enclosure damage, blocked ports — "adaptive excursion control measures the actual excursion of the speaker membrane." This is closed-loop and requires the sense hardware.
- **Feedforward/model-based prediction (RELEVANT):** The underlying CoolFlux DSP runs a Thiele-Small-based excursion model that *predicts* excursion from the input signal, then limits. This is architecturally identical to what the user is building; the sense pins merely correct the model online. The user is effectively running the TFA feedforward predictor without the sense-based correction. TI's PurePath is the same pattern: the excursion is "calculated from the input signal based on the speaker model. Then the excursion will be compared with the maximum excursion before deciding if protection kicks in" (SLAA857).

### Patent landscape

**User's tracked patents (verified bibliographic detail):**
- **US 10,200,003 B2 — "Dynamically extending loudspeaker capabilities."** Assignee **Google LLC**; inventor Gabriel Slotnick; priority **Oct. 3, 2017** (Prov. 62/567,710); granted **Feb. 5, 2019**; active; estimated expiry ~2037–2038 (verify PTA on Google Patents' adjusted-expiration field). Independent claim 1 recites modifying, responsive to a change in available headroom, the Linkwitz-Transform *gain* and *frequency* to extend the loudspeaker's physical LF response. This is volume/headroom-indexed LT scheduling — the closest patent to the user's concept.
- **US 10,666,217 B2 — same title.** Continuation of the above; assignee **Google LLC**; same inventor and Oct. 3, 2017 priority; granted **May 26, 2020**; active. Claim 1 is broader: "monitoring available headroom based on two or more of: an amplifier..., a change in volume..., characteristics of a transducer..., and the audio signal," then modifying equalizer gain to extend LF response. Linkwitz appears in a dependent claim (2nd-order LF shelving EQ), not independent claim 1.
- **US 12,342,139 B2 — "Increasing low frequency extension for microspeakers using a volume dependent Linkwitz transform and multiband compressor."** Assignee **Microsoft Technology Licensing, LLC** — NOT Google; the user's three tracked patents split **2× Google + 1× Microsoft**, correcting any assumption of a single assignee. Inventors Middleton, Venalainen, Borowski; priority **June 3, 2020** (NL 2025740); granted **June 24, 2025**; **active, adjusted expiration explicitly 2041-12-12.** Claim 1 selects a volume-dependent LT + multiband compressor from a **volume-indexed lookup table** (spec: "implemented as a lookup table that is indexed by volume level"), applies the LT to extend LF response, then applies the MBDRC *only* to bands the LT overboosts, less aggressively at higher volume. This is essentially the user's architecture and is the youngest, most directly threatening patent.

**Other relevant patents (assignees, dates, status):**
- **US 8,238,576 B2 — "Level dependent bass management," Cirrus Logic** (Cooper, Melanson, Liu), priority 2005; listed active. Reads on level-dependent bass processing.
- **US 7,171,010 B2 — "Dynamic bass boost apparatus and method," Boston Acoustics** (later D&M Holdings), filed Sept. 2003; status **Expired – Lifetime.** Variable filter driven by signal level. Pre-2006 filing → expired; useful freedom-to-operate prior art.
- **US 2005/0094828 A1 — "Bass boost circuit," New Japan Radio** — signal-level-dependent bass boost that increases gain as bass level drops; old, likely lapsed.
- **EP 2571286 A1** — bass reinforcement computing excursion vs xMAX and amp-saturation-voltage limiting via low-shelf filters; model-based excursion limiting prior art.
- **Harman:** US 11,743,633 (vented-box excursion model from input voltage level + estimated port parameters, applies voltage limit; filed 2022, granted 2023, active); US 10,715,913 (frequency-dependent thermal model for coil temperature, active). Feedforward/model-based.
- **US 11,743,644 B2 — "Managing low frequencies of an output signal," Roku, Inc.** (Curtis, Andersen), filed Dec. 2021, granted Aug. 2023, active. An excursion predictor from measured SPL/voltage feeds a cutoff-frequency selector that dynamically selects the dynamic high-pass corner — level/excursion-dependent high-pass corner scheduling (semi-sensing).
- **Bose family: US 9,729,986 ("Protection of a speaker using temperature calibration") / US 10,015,608.** These describe a level-dependent high-pass corner that jumps up above an amplitude limit and relaxes below. Concretely (US 9,729,986, FIG. 23B), the cutoff is "held at 800 Hz" then decreases "in a stepwise fashion at set cutoff frequency intervals per unit time (e.g., **25 Hz/ms, 100 Hz/second**)... from approximately 800 Hz to approximately 600 Hz." This is a rare *published, concrete corner-slew-rate number* and is directly applicable as a design reference (and as prior art). Bose assignee attribution should still be confirmed on Google Patents before relying on it in a filing.
- **General excursion-prediction/protection prior art:** US 10,893,359 / US 11,234,075 (excursion prediction with LF + transient protection and lookahead delay); US 10,194,241 (non-linear constraint filter with distortion suppression); US 10,536,774 / US 10,349,195 (constrained nonlinear parameter estimation for smart limiting); US 9,516,443 (non-linear control of loudspeakers); US 2022/0038815 (excursion protection with laser characterization); US 12,231,860 (measurement-based excursion limiting with slew-rate limiting). Several are model/measurement-based and on-point as evidence the technique is well-known.

**Interpretation for the user:** The concept space (volume-indexed LT scheduling, level-dependent moving high-pass, model-based excursion limiting) is densely patented, and the youngest relevant patent (Microsoft US 12,342,139) runs to ~2041. However, foundational level-dependent bass-boost patents (Boston Acoustics US 7,171,010) are expired, and the general model-based excursion-prediction technique is old and widely practiced. Design freedom lies in (a) per-*unit* commissioning rather than per-model databases (differentiates from SAM, TI, Harman), (b) target-*selection* scheduling rather than a runtime adaptive compressor (differentiates from the Microsoft MBDRC claim), and (c) an open-source, non-sensing feedforward implementation.

### Transition dynamics — published guidance
- **Attack/release asymmetry and pumping/breathing.** The Analog Devices/Maxim ALC app note ("ALC Improves Sound Quality While Protecting Speakers") is explicit: "short time constants cause the ALC to rapidly follow changing signal levels and thus protect against even the shortest spikes... but it can also allow audible artifacts like 'pumping' and 'breathing' as the gain rapidly adjusts," while "longer time constants prevent artifacts and maximize the sound quality." The universal design rule: fast attack to catch excursion, slow release to stay inaudible.
- **Sliding high-pass excursion limiting (most directly applicable).** Linea Research's "Setting Up Limiters" app note codifies exactly the mechanism the user should emulate: rather than varying gain, it "uses a sliding high-pass filter to progressively curtail the low-frequency response, effectively limiting the linear excursion to below the X-max specification," set from "the family of Excursion vs. Frequency curves of the driver for various drive voltage levels." Its release/cool-down time constant is expressed as a *multiple of attack*: "A value of 2.0X will suit most applications. A value of 3.0X would offer better protection." This is the pro-audio codification of the user's approach and supplies concrete asymmetry-ratio guidance.
- **Lookahead.** Speaker-protection patents (US 10,893,359 / 11,234,075) and the diyAudio sensorless limiter both use a lookahead delay so gain/corner changes complete before cone inertia makes them ineffective; the lookahead can be short because the transient stage's input is already high-passed.
- **Excursion-vs-frequency physics the schedule must respect.** Excursion rises 4× per octave downward, and each doubling of level (+6 dB) doubles required excursion; an LT extending an octave below the natural corner demands ~12 dB of gain and ~4× the excursion — which is precisely why the corner must retreat as level rises (diyAudio LT threads; ESP Project 71).
- **Human audibility of slow spectral tilt / LF level modulation.** No single authoritative published JND figure for slow bass-shelf modulation was located; this remains the thinnest-sourced area and an open item. Prevailing engineering practice (ADI, Linea) treats multi-hundred-ms releases and slow, small gain changes as safe from pumping, but a rigorous psychoacoustic threshold citation is lacking.

## Recommendations

**Recommended parameter envelope for the volume-indexed target-selection scheduler** (grounded in what ships):

1. **Schedule by volume rungs, not continuous per-sample adaptation.** Use a lookup table of pre-computed biquad target sets indexed by attenuator position (mirrors Microsoft US 12,342,139's lookup-table architecture and Sonos's volume indexing). Because this treads on the Microsoft claim, keep per-*unit* commissioning and target-selection (no in-line MBDRC) as your explicit differentiators.
2. **Step size:** keep LF shelf/boost changes small — on the order of 1–1.5 dB per rung — to stay below the audibility of a sudden tilt shift. Sonos's perceived "on/off" artifact is the cautionary example of steps that are too coarse. If you also move the corner, cap the per-rung corner move well under the Bose-documented protection slew (25 Hz/ms) so a *scheduled* step is far gentler than an *emergency* one.
3. **Dwell/hysteresis:** require the volume to hold a new rung for a dwell interval before switching targets, and use asymmetric thresholds (larger gap to step *up* in extension than to step *down*) to prevent hunting at rung boundaries.
4. **Up-rate vs down-rate asymmetry:** retreat (reduce extension / raise corner) fast and extend (add bass) slowly. Target a fast protection direction (tens of milliseconds) and a slow restoration (hundreds of ms to a couple of seconds), consistent with the ADI pumping guidance and Linea's 2–3× cool-down multiple of attack.
5. **Coefficient-change smoothing:** never hard-swap biquad coefficients. Crossfade old→new coefficient sets over a few tens of milliseconds (equal-power crossfade of two parallel filter instances), or interpolate coefficients along a smooth trajectory, to avoid transient clicks/zipper noise from a discontinuous state change.
6. **Keep a short lookahead delay** sized to the lowest passband period so corner moves complete ahead of cone inertia; the emergency transient stage can be shorter because its input is already high-passed.
7. **Commission per-unit excursion ceilings** from measured excursion-vs-frequency-vs-drive-level families (as Linea recommends and as SAM/TI do per-model). This is your legitimate technical and IP differentiator.

**Benchmarks/thresholds that would change these recommendations:**
- If listening tests reveal audible pumping on sustained bass, *lengthen release* first and *reduce step size* second.
- If protection lags real excursion risk (audible distortion or bottoming on transients), *shorten attack/lookahead* and add a faster transient-excursion stage (per the US 10,893,359 pattern).
- If rung-boundary hunting is audible, *increase hysteresis/dwell*.

**IP action:** obtain a professional freedom-to-operate opinion specifically against Microsoft US 12,342,139 (active to Dec. 2041) and Google US 10,200,003 / 10,666,217. Document per-unit commissioning and non-adaptive target-selection as distinguishing features, and rely on expired foundations (Boston Acoustics US 7,171,010) as prior-art support.

## Caveats
- **Marketing vs substantiation:** KEF MIE/iBX claims are marketing-only for engineering purposes — no published transfer schedule, corner numbers, or time constants. B&O, Devialet, TI, NXP, and Dynaudio have manufacturer-technical or independent-measurement backing.
- **Conflicts flagged:** (a) Sonos documents *continuous* volume-dependent loudness, but users and reportedly a Sonos rep describe it as effectively on/off — unresolved, and a direct warning about coarse stepping. (b) B&O ABL is *signal-dependent* (not volume-dependent) per B&O, contrasting with the user's volume-indexed approach — they mitigate the same risk with different control variables.
- **Sensing vs feedforward:** NXP/Goodix TFA and TI parts are frequently described in press as "measuring" excursion; the runtime *sensing* modes are not usable by the user, but the *model-prediction* path is. This distinction is real and load-bearing for relevance.
- **Numbers are scarce in hi-fi, abundant in silicon:** almost no consumer loudspeaker vendor publishes corner-vs-level tables, step sizes, or time constants. The concrete numbers here come from TI datasheets, Linea Research, Analog Devices/Maxim, and the Bose patent — semiconductor/pro sources — and must be adapted to the user's specific driver.
- **Thinnest area:** published human-audibility JND thresholds for slow LF spectral-tilt modulation were not located; the rate guidance here is engineering practice, not a psychoacoustic threshold citation.
- **Patent expiry precision:** only Microsoft US 12,342,139's adjusted expiration (2041-12-12) is explicitly confirmed; Google patent expiries (~2037–2038) are inferred from filing chains and need PTA verification. The Bose attribution for US 9,729,986 / 10,015,608 is unverified pending a check of the assignee field.
