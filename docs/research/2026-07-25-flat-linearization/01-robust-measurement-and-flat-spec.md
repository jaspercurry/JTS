# Robust Consumer-Grade Loudspeaker Measurement Under Early Boundary Reflections, and Defining "Flat" for an Auto-Calibrating DSP Speaker

> Owner-commissioned deep-research report, delivered 2026-07-25. Preserved
> verbatim (primary source); the adopted synthesis lives in
> [`flat-linearization-plan.md`](../../flat-linearization-plan.md), which
> wins where they disagree. Known errata are listed in this directory's
> [README](README.md).

**TL;DR**
- No shipped consumer product solves your exact problem — removing a τ≈0.3 ms boundary bounce from a *single* on-axis anechoic-direct-sound measurement. Every mass-market system (Sonos, Dirac, Audyssey, Genelec, Trinnov, Lyngdorf, Apple) *averages the interference away* using spatial diversity (multiple points or a moving mic), combines captures as a **power spectral density (energy) average**, and none tries to parametrically subtract the bounce.
- The right combining estimator is a **power (energy) mean of many spatially-decorrelated captures**, not max-hold or complex averaging. Cook's sinc(kr) law says positions decorrelate a comb null at ~λ/2 (≈10 cm at 1.7 kHz, ≈2 cm at 8.6 kHz); ~9–10 independent captures give ≈±1 dB at 1 kHz with 1/6-oct smoothing. Cepstral/homomorphic echo removal is **academic/cross-check only** — not production-proven for consumer directivity-weighted bounces.
- "Flat" should be specified as the **CTA-2034 Listening Window** (spatial average of nine responses over ±10° vertical × ±30° horizontal), the direct-sound metric that best predicts preference in the Olive model. Use a defensible spec of **±1.5–2 dB, 1/3-oct-smoothed, ~200 Hz–8 kHz**, relax to best-effort above 8 kHz where UMIK-2 unit-to-unit uncertainty alone reaches 3 dB, and hard-cut the pass/fail metric at 16 kHz. Do NOT equalize narrow non-minimum-phase interference dips.

## QUESTION 1 — How shipped auto-calibration products neutralize early boundary reflections

**(a) Consensus answer.** The universal shipped strategy is *spatial averaging*, not echo removal. Products capture the response at many points (or continuously while the mic moves), convert each capture to a power spectrum, and average the power spectra. Because interference nulls move with position while the true direct/resonant response does not, the moving nulls partially wash out while the stable response survives. Crucially, nearly all consumer systems then build a **minimum-phase** correction only, deliberately declining to "correct" non-minimum-phase interference notches.

- **Sonos Trueplay** — The mic (iOS device) is *moved* continuously ("the room dance"). The test tone is periodic (~1/3 s period for a mono product); the app "frames" the incoming mic signal once per period, extracts an impulse response per frame, checks each frame's SNR and discards bad ones, and confirms motion via accelerometer. Over a 45-second mono sweep this yields **"more than 150" measured locations**. The combine step is explicit in Sonos's own engineering blog, verbatim: *"It turns out there's really no such thing as an 'average impulse response.' It's different at every point and won't average very well at all. So, each impulse response is turned into a power spectral density (PSD). These average nicely… and the result is a pretty good representation of what you'll hear in most places in the room."* So Trueplay uses a **spatial power (PSD) average**, with an extra correction to avoid over-weighting frames captured very close to the speaker. Phase is discarded (*"For the spectral correction, we don't use the phase information"*), correction is fit to an in-room target (not flat), realized as a **16-filter IIR/PEQ** via gradient descent, with per-model boost limits. On why it stays minimum-phase, Sonos is explicit: *"Correcting the room's nonminimum phase degradations is also possible to some degree, but only in one location. When that's done the sound tends to degrade even more than it already was in other parts of the room—even in locations quite close to the optimized one."* What the motion buys: it converts a single-point comb (which would be "baked in") into a spatial ensemble whose interference nulls decorrelate. What it deliberately does NOT correct: room non-minimum-phase behavior, phase, and it targets an in-room curve rather than flat.
- **Dirac Live** — Recommends **9 positions** ("spread out… as randomly as possible"); miniDSP's guidance offers 9 (tight), 13, or 17 positions arranged in a grid around the seat. It builds a **mixed-phase** (FIR+IIR) model from the multi-point set — unusual in that it corrects some time-domain/phase behavior, not just magnitude — and normally limits boost to ~10 dB. Dirac explicitly does NOT try to fill non-minimum-phase cancellation dips (a reflection-cancellation null "no amount of correction gain… will fill").
- **Audyssey MultEQ** — Up to **8 positions**. The distinctive combine is **fuzzy c-means clustering** (Bharitkar & Kyriakakis, US Patent 8,005,228, assigned to Audyssey): group the position responses into clusters by similarity, derive prototype responses, combine into a general point response, then invert its minimum-phase part. This weights positions by cluster membership rather than a flat average.
- **Genelec GLM / AutoCal & AutoCal 2** — Professional/prosumer; a **single mic position** for the basic case (unlimited positions optional). AutoCal 2's headline claim is "faster and more accurate discrimination between direct and reflected sound." Uses **AccuSmooth** frequency-dependent smoothing (narrower bandwidth at LF, ~1/3-oct at HF). Targets flat with scaled smoothing.
- **Trinnov Optimizer** — Uses a **4-capsule tetrahedral 3D microphone** (founders Arnaud Laborie, Rémy Bruno, Sébastien Montoya, company founded 2003; each capsule individually factory-calibrated) that localizes each speaker in 3D by arrival-time triangulation and separately identifies "active crossovers, loudspeaker response, early reflections, room modes and power response." Typically **one measurement point** (pros) or a few. This is the one shipped system that *spatially resolves* reflections (via the capsule array) rather than only averaging them out — but it uses a dedicated 4-capsule mic, not a single consumer mic.
- **Apple HomePod** — Uses onboard mics to "listen for sound reflections" to determine whether it is against a wall/free-standing and adapts beamforming and EQ in real time. This is coarse boundary *classification*, not a measured transfer-function inversion. No detailed public algorithmic disclosure; marketing-level only.
- **Lyngdorf RoomPerfect** — A **Focus** measurement at the seat (mic pointed at the speakers, minimizing room contribution) plus random "room knowledge" measurements until an information metric reaches ~95%; in practice 5–7 measurements. Explicitly separates speaker sound from room and "only correct[s] what needs to be corrected."

**(b) Shipped-product evidence vs academic-only.** Shipped and technically disclosed: Sonos PSD power averaging + moving mic (engineering blog + patent family around US 10,045,138 "Hybrid Test Tone for Space-Averaged Room Audio Calibration Using a Moving Microphone"); Audyssey fuzzy c-means (granted patent 8,005,228); Dirac mixed-phase multipoint; Trinnov 4-capsule spatial method. Marketing-only (no algorithm disclosed): Apple HomePod room sensing. **No shipped product does single-capture parametric boundary-bounce removal.** The closest is Trinnov, which spatially *resolves* reflections with a special mic rather than modeling/subtracting a bounce from a single consumer-mic capture.

**(c) Citations.** Sonos Tech Blog, "Trueplay Spectral Correction"; US 10,045,138 (Sonos, moving-microphone space-averaged calibration); US 8,005,228 (Audyssey/Bharitkar & Kyriakakis, fuzzy c-means multi-listener correction); miniDSP SHD Dirac Live manual (9/13/17 positions); Audioholics interview with Mathias Johansson (Dirac, 9 positions, mixed-phase, 10 dB boost cap); Genelec GLM/AutoCal 2 (audioXpress, Genelec.com); Trinnov Optimizer manual & technology pages, Sound On Sound (founders/history); Apple HomePod newsroom/product page; Lyngdorf RoomPerfect (AVForums, LowBeats, Steinway Lyngdorf).

**(d) Confidence + contested.** High confidence on the "spatial averaging + minimum-phase" consensus and on Sonos/Audyssey mechanics (primary sources). Medium on exact position-combining math for Dirac/Genelec/Trinnov (patents describe families; product specifics are partly proprietary). Contested/opaque: HomePod's actual processing.

**(e) Design consequence.** Adopt the industry-proven path — capture many spatially-diverse points (or a moving-mic sweep), combine as a **power/PSD average**, and correct **minimum-phase only** — rather than trying to remove the bounce from one on-axis capture.

## QUESTION 2 — Direct-sound estimation from N spatially-diverse captures: the right combining estimator

**(a) Consensus answer with numbers.**
- **Estimator choice.** Use a **power (energy) average** of magnitude spectra across decorrelated positions. Rationale from the averaging theory: coherent (identical) signal content adds "on a signal basis" (+6 dB per doubling) while uncorrelated content adds "on a power basis" (+3 dB per doubling) (Struck, "Time Synchronous Averaging," audioXpress/Voice Coil 2012; Müller & Massarani, JAES 2001). A **complex (vector) average** would let the *moving* nulls cancel truthfully and reject interference best — but it requires phase coherence and precise per-capture time alignment you will not reliably get from a hand-moved consumer mic, so it is not robust for your UX. A **log-magnitude (dB) average** and a **power average** both "fill in" moving nulls (a *positive* bias in the dips), which is actually desirable here because it pushes the estimate toward the true direct response and away from the position-specific cancellation. **Max-hold/upper-envelope is positively biased by noise and peaks** (order-statistics: the maximum of M noisy samples over-reads), so it over-corrects; avoid it as the primary estimator. **Median** is robust to individual deep nulls (rejects outliers) and is a good cross-check, but lacks a published dB-bias characterization for the comb case.
- **How far apart, how many.** The spatial correlation of a random/reverberant field is Cook's law, **R = sin(kr)/kr** (Cook et al., JASA 1955; verified by Jacobsen & Roisin, JASA 2000). First zero at **kr = π → r = λ/2**. For your comb nulls: decorrelation distance ≈ **10 cm at 1.7 kHz, 3.4 cm at 5.1 kHz, 2.0 cm at 8.6 kHz, 1.4 cm at 12 kHz, 1.1 cm at 15.5 kHz**. So a few cm of mic movement randomizes the HF nulls, but you need ~10 cm of spread to decorrelate the lowest (1.7 kHz) null. Below ~1 kHz decorrelation distances grow to tens of cm (≈17 cm at 1 kHz, ≈34 cm at 500 Hz).
- **Accuracy vs N.** Standard error falls as **1/√N** independent samples (σ = 11.14/(√(1+2.38·B·Tr)·√N); Ohl, MMM paper 2014, after Schroeder/Hopkins). Worked example at 1 kHz, RT 0.3 s (close to your small room): with 1/6-oct smoothing you need **~10 independent measurements to reach ±1 dB**; at 100 Hz ~9 independent measurements gets you only to ±2 dB. Harman's own patent work states verbatim (US 8,130,966): *"Research shows that four farfield locations are ideal from a practical point of view; five or more locations do not provide a significant improvement in response."* So for **±1 dB over 300 Hz–16 kHz**, target on the order of **8–12 decorrelated captures**, with the spread governed by the *lowest* frequency you care about (≥~10 cm spread for 1.7 kHz; ≥~30 cm for 300–500 Hz).

**(b) Shipped-product evidence vs academic-only.** Shipped: Sonos (>150 moving-mic PSD samples), Dirac (9), Audyssey (8, fuzzy-c-means-weighted), Harman patent's "4 far-field points." Academic/first-principles: the exact dB bias of power-mean vs median vs max-hold against a *moving comb null* is **not tabulated in any single primary source** — the *direction* of each bias is established (power/log fill nulls; complex cancels them; max-hold positively biased) but the comb-specific dB figures are first-principles. Cook's sinc(kr) and the 1/√N law are solid primary sources.

**(c) Citations.** Cook, Waterhouse, Berendt, Edelman, Thompson, "Measurement of Correlation Coefficients in Reverberant Sound Fields," JASA 27(6):1072, 1955 (DOI 10.1121/1.1908122); Jacobsen & Roisin, "The coherence of reverberant sound fields," JASA 108(1):204, 2000 (DOI 10.1121/1.429457); Struck, "Time Synchronous Averaging," audioXpress 2012; Müller & Massarani, "Transfer-Function Measurement with Sweeps," JAES 49(6):443, 2001; Ohl, "Back to pink? MMM Moving Mic Measurement," 2014; US 8,130,966 (Harman); US 8,280,076 (Harman, spatial σ/envelope example); Lundeby et al., "Uncertainties of Measurements in Room Acoustics," Acustica 1995.

**(d) Confidence + contested.** High on sinc(kr) decorrelation and 1/√N. Medium on the precise N for ±1 dB (depends on smoothing fraction and how "independent" your hand-moved samples truly are). Contested/unpublished: exact estimator-bias dB figures for a comb null — you will have to characterize this empirically on your own rig.

**(e) Design consequence.** Combine ~8–12 captures spread ≥10 cm (≥30 cm if you want ±1 dB down to 300 Hz) as a **power average**; keep **median as an automated sanity cross-check** and never use max-hold as the primary estimator.

## QUESTION 3 — Single-capture echo removal: is cepstral/homomorphic or parametric subtraction production-viable?

**(a) Consensus answer.** The math is well established and old: complex-cepstrum/homomorphic deconvolution maps the convolutional echo into an additive, isolated peak in the cepstral (quefrency) domain, which can be liftered out (Oppenheim & Schafer; Bolt & MacDonald; Kemerait & Childers). For a *single discrete echo of known τ and r*, you can also parametrically deconvolve the known comb |1 + r·e^{-jωτ}|. In controlled conditions (electrical analogue, known delay, high SNR) cepstral reflection extraction works. **But** the literature repeatedly flags fragility: phase distortion in the channel "precipitates the deterioration of the complex cepstrum" (so the *power* cepstrum is often recommended), and success requires careful choice of signal, sampling, echo delay vs total window, time-domain averaging, and zero-padding. In loudspeaker practice the established, robust alternative is **time gating** or **frequency-dependent windowing (FDW)** — but your τ≈0.3 ms echo is far too early to gate without destroying resolution below ~3 kHz (your own constraint), and FDW at those frequencies would likewise smooth away the very band you need.

**Production robustness failure modes for your case:**
- **Frequency-dependent r (directivity-weighted bounce).** A compression-driver horn's output into the bounce surface is highly directional, so r is strongly frequency-dependent — the echo is *not* a clean scaled-and-delayed copy, so the cepstral peak smears and a single-(τ,r) parametric model is misspecified. This is the biggest killer.
- **Two nearby echoes** (your 2τ/3τ repeats) produce interacting cepstral peaks/rahmonics that are hard to lifter cleanly.
- **Phone-mic SNR and phase behavior** degrade the complex cepstrum exactly where it is most needed.

**(b) Shipped-product evidence vs academic-only.** **Academic-only for the reflection-removal use.** No shipped consumer auto-calibration product is documented to perform cepstral or parametric single-echo subtraction on its measurement. Cepstral/homomorphic techniques appear in speech dereverberation and in communications equalizers (e.g., minimum-phase extraction via homomorphic deconvolution in a whitening matched filter, US 6,862,326), and cepstral reflectometry appears in acoustics research — but not as a shipped loudspeaker-calibration reflection remover. The loudspeaker-industry production tools for reflection handling are **gating/FDW** (REW, ARTA, Acourate, VituixCAD merging), which do not apply to a 0.3 ms echo.

**(c) Citations.** Oppenheim & Schafer homomorphic deconvolution (MIT); Bees, "Application of Complex Cepstrum to Acoustic Dereverberation" (McGill); Kemerait & Childers, "Signal detection and extraction by cepstrum techniques"; "Homomorphic deconvolution in reverberant and distortional channels" (JSV 1978, power-cepstrum recommendation); US 6,862,326 (homomorphic minimum-phase extraction); Acourate/REW FDW documentation; VituixCAD/ARTA gating-and-merge practice.

**(d) Confidence + contested.** High confidence that this is not production-proven for consumer reflection removal and that a directivity-weighted, multi-echo, phone-SNR case is exactly where cepstral methods are known to fail. Contested: whether a *constrained* parametric fit (estimate τ from the cepstrum, fit a frequency-dependent r) could work as a *seed* for your spatial-average estimator — plausible but unproven.

**(e) Design consequence.** Treat cepstral/parametric echo removal as a **cross-check / seed only** (use the cepstrum to *detect* τ and flag the bounce), and make the **spatial power average the primary direct-sound estimator**; do not ship single-capture echo subtraction as the primary method.

## QUESTION 4 — What "flat" should mean, per standards and psychoacoustics

**(a) Consensus answer.**
- **CTA/CEA-2034 definitions.** The **On-Axis** response is the 0° reference. The **Listening Window (LW)** is a **spatial average of nine responses in the ±10° vertical and ±30° horizontal range** (i.e., 0°, ±10/±20/±30° horizontal and ±10° vertical). The averaging is a magnitude/power spatial average, not a complex average, and by being a spatial average it "attenuates small fluctuations that are merely the result of acoustical interference" while retaining resonances. Other curves: **Early Reflections** (weighted estimate of first bounces — floor 20/30/40° down, ceiling 40/50/60° up, walls per the standard), **Sound Power** (weighted RMS over the full sphere every 10°), **Predicted In-Room Response (PIR)** (weighted sum of LW + early reflections + sound power), and the two **Directivity Indexes** (SPDI = LW − Sound Power; ERDI = LW − Early Reflections).
- **Which correlates best with preference.** In the **Olive multiple-regression preference model** (Olive, AES 116 paper 6113 & AES 117 paper 6190, 2004; US Patent 8,311,232), the predictor variables are **AAD/ADDON (absolute average deviation of on-axis), LFX (low-frequency extension), LFQ (low-frequency quality), SMON (smoothness of on-axis), and SMSP (smoothness of sound power)**, combined (verbatim from the patent) as **Pref = 6.04 − 0.67·ADDON − 1.28·LFX − 0.66·LFQ + 4.02·SMON + 3.58·SMSP**. Two facts matter for you: (1) **smoothness (SMON, SMSP) carries the largest positive weights and low-frequency extension the largest single magnitude**, and (2) Toole/Olive practice holds the **Listening Window** as the single most important direct-sound tonal curve — flatness/smoothness of the LW is the target for an anechoic-ish direct-sound spec. So: **spec the Listening Window flat/smooth**, not just the raw 0° on-axis.
- **Defensible tolerance windows.** Credible manufacturer specs cluster at **±1 to ±3 dB with 1/3-oct (or finer at LF) smoothing**: Genelec states verbatim that its product specs use *"± 1 dB, ± 2 dB, ± 2.5 dB, ± 3 dB"* (free-field, anechoic) and uses AccuSmooth (finer than 1/3-oct at LF, ~1/3-oct at HF); Neumann KH120 quotes ±3 dB (52 Hz–21 kHz) free-field; Stereophile routinely holds good monitors to "±1.5 dB, 300 Hz–20 kHz" on a 30°-averaged response. A tolerance **tighter than ±2 dB above 8 kHz is not meaningful for a UMIK-2-class mic**: real-world UMIK-2 owners report units disagreeing by *"2 to 3 dB… 3dB lower above 2kHz than the other,"* i.e. ~3 dB systematic unit-to-unit uncertainty from 2 kHz upward, and any single unit's HF calibration correction (4–5 dB near the band edges) carries substantial residual uncertainty. So a defensible spec is **±1.5 dB, 200 Hz–2 kHz; ±2 dB, 2–8 kHz; ±2.5–3 dB, 8–16 kHz**, 1/3-oct smoothed, on the LW.
- **What NOT to equalize.** Established Toole doctrine: EQ **minimum-phase resonances** (which radiate similarly over wide angles and appear in both on-axis and off-axis/spatial-average curves), but do **NOT** EQ **narrow non-minimum-phase acoustical-interference dips** (position-dependent cancellations) or **diffraction ripple** — "If they 'see' a ripple in a measured curve caused by acoustical interference of direct and reflected sound, and try to flatten it, they may be adding a resonance and degrading a good loudspeaker." Peaks EQ well; sharp position-sensitive nulls do not. Exclude such features from BOTH the correction filter AND the pass/fail metric — operationalize this by only correcting features that survive spatial averaging and appear across the off-axis family, and by cut-only correction of peaks with capped boost of dips.
- **Above 16 kHz.** Industry practice is **disclosed best-effort or a hard spec cutoff**: publish a spec band (e.g., ±X dB to 16 kHz) and treat everything above as "best effort, not guaranteed" because mic confidence collapses there.

**(b) Shipped-product/standard evidence vs academic.** Standard/primary: CTA-2034-A/-B definitions; Olive patent 8,311,232 and JAES 2004 papers; Genelec/Neumann published tolerances. Academic/practitioner doctrine: Toole *Sound Reproduction* on resonances vs interference. All well-sourced.

**(c) Citations.** ANSI/CTA-2034-A (R-2020) & -B (2024); Devantier, "Characterizing the Amplitude Response of Loudspeaker Systems," AES 113 (2002), paper 5638; Olive, "A Multiple Regression Model…," AES 116 (paper 6113) & 117 (paper 6190), 2004; US Patent 8,311,232 (Olive/Harman); Toole, *Sound Reproduction*, 3rd ed. (2017); Genelec technical glossary (tolerances, AccuSmooth); Neumann KH120 spec; Stereophile Genelec G Three measurements (±1.5 dB, 30° average); UMIK-2 calibration-uncertainty reports (ASR, miniDSP forum).

**(d) Confidence + contested.** High on CTA-2034 definitions and Olive weights (primary). High that sub-±2 dB above 8 kHz is not meaningful with a UMIK-2. Contested: exact target *shape* (flat vs gently downward-tilted "in-room" target) — that is a target-curve-layer decision you already own; the *direct-sound* spec should be flat/smooth LW.

**(e) Design consequence.** Write the pass/fail spec against the **CTA-2034 Listening Window** (nine-angle spatial average), **±1.5 dB 200 Hz–2 kHz / ±2 dB 2–8 kHz / ±2.5–3 dB 8–16 kHz, 1/3-oct smoothed**, correcting only spatially-robust minimum-phase features and declaring >16 kHz best-effort.

## QUESTION 5 — Closed-loop measure–correct–remeasure practice

**(a) Consensus answer.** Most shipped consumer systems are **open-loop single-pass**: measure → compute filters → apply, with no automatic re-measure/verify. The professional/measurement world does close the loop, usually manually:
- **REW workflow** (the de-facto DIY standard): measure → generate filters → *predicted* response is shown → apply → **re-measure to verify predicted vs actual** → iterate. Convergence is by inspection; 1–2 verification passes typical.
- **Lyngdorf RoomPerfect** is *iterative by construction*: it accumulates measurements until a "room knowledge" information metric reaches ~90–98%, which is a convergence criterion on measurement sufficiency (not a correction re-measure). Typically converges in 5–7 measurements.
- **Genelec GLM/AutoCal 2** references a cloud database of thousands of rooms and computes in one pass; "AutoCal 2… faster and more accurate discrimination between direct and reflected sound." No public evidence of an automatic correct-then-remeasure loop, though the workflow allows re-running.
- **Dirac Live** shows a predicted post-correction curve; verification is a manual re-measure. No documented automatic iterate-to-convergence.
- **Divergence/rollback:** Sonos exposes a simple **on/off toggle and re-run** (user-level rollback); it caps boost per model and protects the driver rather than iterating. No consumer system publicly documents automatic divergence detection with rollback.
- **Predicted-vs-remeasured agreement** and the **under-delivered-boost** phenomenon: your observation that commanded lift falls ~2–3 dB short near driver limits is explained by **thermal power compression** — as the voice coil heats, its DC resistance rises. Per the physics literature (Wikipedia/Stereophile 2006), *"A voice coil made of copper wire will have its DC resistance increase by about 72% when heating up from 20 °C… to 200 °C, and its sensitivity will decrease by 4.7 decibels."* So a boost that raises drive also raises dissipation and the realized SPL saturates. This is level- and time-dependent and **only a re-measure at the target playback level catches it**. In multiway systems, coil heating also shifts effective crossover summation, adding further error. This strongly motivates a closed loop for you specifically.

**(b) Shipped-product evidence vs academic.** Shipped/documented: RoomPerfect "room knowledge" convergence metric; Sonos toggle/re-run; GLM one-pass + cloud. Practitioner: REW predicted-vs-measured verify loop. Physics (well-sourced): power compression (Wikipedia/JBL, thermal-model patents e.g. US 9,414,161 which uses probe tones to track voice-coil resistance; US 7,113,603 thermal overload control). Academic: control-theory treatment of iterative EQ convergence exists but is not the basis of shipped consumer products. **No shipped consumer product publicly reports quantitative predicted-vs-remeasured agreement figures** — that is a gap.

**(c) Citations.** REW documentation & community verify-loop practice; Lyngdorf RoomPerfect (AVForums, LowBeats, Steinway Lyngdorf — room-knowledge %); Genelec GLM/AutoCal 2 (audioXpress, Genelec); Wikipedia "Power compression" (72% Re rise, 4.7 dB loss, citing Stereophile 2006); US 9,414,161 (voice-coil thermal model with probe tones); US 7,113,603 (thermal overload control).

**(d) Confidence + contested.** High that consumer systems are largely open-loop and that power compression explains your 2–3 dB shortfall. Contested/unavailable: quantitative predicted-vs-remeasured accuracy from any vendor (not published).

**(e) Design consequence.** Since you own the whole chain and see a real 2–3 dB compression-driven shortfall, **implement an explicit closed loop**: measure → correct → **re-measure at target SPL** → apply a residual-error trim, with a convergence threshold (e.g., stop when residual < ~1 dB RMS over 300 Hz–8 kHz) and a rollback if a pass increases error.

## Recommendations (staged, with thresholds)

1. **Primary architecture (adopt now):** Replace single-point on-axis measurement with a **guided multi-capture spatial power average** — either discrete points or a Trueplay-style moving-mic sweep — combined as a **PSD (energy) average**, correcting **minimum-phase only, cut-biased**. This is the only approach proven at consumer scale. *Threshold to keep it:* if a validation rig shows the spatial average recovers the anechoic LW to ±1 dB (300 Hz–8 kHz), ship it.
2. **Capture geometry:** Require ≥8–12 decorrelated captures; instruct the user to move the phone over ≥~10 cm for HF and ≥~30 cm of spread to pin down 300–500 Hz. Use accelerometer/motion checks and per-frame SNR gating exactly as Sonos does. *If* you can only get 3–4 captures (poor UX compliance), expect ±2 dB at best and widen the spec accordingly.
3. **Estimator:** Power mean primary; **median as an automated cross-check** (flag frequencies where the two disagree by >2 dB as interference-dominated and *exclude them from correction and pass/fail*). Do not use max-hold.
4. **Cepstral method:** Use only to **detect** τ (confirm the bounce, estimate its delay for diagnostics/UX warnings), never as the primary corrector. Revisit only if you move to a fixed cradle geometry where τ and r are stable and directivity-weighting can be calibrated out.
5. **Spec:** Define "flat" as the **CTA-2034 Listening Window**, tolerance **±1.5 dB (200 Hz–2 kHz) / ±2 dB (2–8 kHz) / ±2.5–3 dB (8–16 kHz), 1/3-oct smoothed**; >16 kHz best-effort. Exclude non-minimum-phase interference dips and diffraction ripple from both correction and metric.
6. **Closed loop:** Add a **re-measure pass at target SPL**; converge when residual < ~1 dB RMS (300 Hz–8 kHz); roll back any pass that increases error. This is the only way to catch your power-compression shortfall.

## Caveats
- Your **exact τ≈0.3 ms single-capture case is not addressed by any shipped product or by a dedicated published study** — all shipped systems sidestep it by spatial averaging, and the cepstral literature does not cover a directivity-weighted multi-echo consumer bounce. Treat any single-capture removal as experimental.
- The **estimator-bias dB figures** (power vs median vs max-hold near a comb null) are **first-principles, not from a single primary source** — characterize them empirically on your rig.
- **Vendor algorithm details** (Dirac/Genelec/Trinnov/Apple internal combining and any verification loop) are partly proprietary; several claims (esp. HomePod) are marketing, not technical disclosure.
- **No manufacturer publishes predicted-vs-remeasured agreement numbers**, so your closed-loop accuracy target must be set from your own data.
- **Mic uncertainty dominates >8 kHz** (≈3 dB unit-to-unit on UMIK-2 above 2 kHz); do not over-spec there.
